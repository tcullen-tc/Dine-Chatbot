================================================================================
DINÉ ECONOMY: TRADITIONAL ECONOMY, TRADING POSTS, MODERN DEVELOPMENT, AND CHALLENGES
================================================================================
Sources: Navajo Nation Division of Economic Development, "Navajo Trading" (Willow Roberts Powers), "The Navajo Economy" (Navajo Nation)


PART 1: TRADITIONAL ECONOMY (Pre-Spanish Contact)
================================================================================

Before European contact, the Diné economy was based on hunting, gathering, and small-scale agriculture.

HUNTING:

Primary game animals:
- Deer (most important)
- Antelope
- Rabbit
- Prairie dog
- Mountain sheep (rare)
- Elk (rare)

Hunting practices:
- Communal hunts (group effort)
- Respect for animal spirits
- Offerings to Holy People
- Meat shared with community
- Hides used for clothing

GATHERING:

Plants gathered:
- Pinon nuts (most important gathered food)
- Wild greens (spring)
- Berries (summer)
- Cactus fruits (prickly pear)
- Seeds (various)
- Wild onions
- Yucca fruit

Gathering practices:
- Women primarily gathered
- Seasonal rounds (following food sources)
- Sustainable harvesting (take only what needed)
- Sharing with community

AGRICULTURE:

Crops grown:
- Corn (most important)
- Beans
- Squash
- Sunflowers (seeds)
- Tobacco (ceremonial)

Farming methods:
- Dry farming (no irrigation)
- Flood farming (planting in washes)
- Small fields (not large-scale)
- Women primarily farmed
- Shared harvest

WEALTH:

Traditional wealth was measured by:
- Knowledge (ceremonial, hunting, gathering)
- Skills (weaving, hunting, farming)
- Community respect
- Generosity (giving to others)
- Family connections (K'é)

Not material possessions (land ownership was not individual)


PART 2: SHEEP ECONOMY (Post-Spanish Contact)
================================================================================

Sheep transformed the Diné economy after Spanish contact.

INTRODUCTION OF SHEEP (1600s):

- Spanish brought sheep to the Southwest
- Navajo adopted sheep herding quickly
- Sheep adapted well to Navajo environment
- Sheep became central to economy and culture

SHEEP AS WEALTH:

A man's wealth was measured in sheep:
- Poor: 0-50 sheep
- Average: 50-200 sheep
- Rich: 200-1,000+ sheep

Sheep as currency:
- Sheep traded for goods
- Sheep given as gifts (strengthened K'é)
- Sheep used as bride price
- Sheep sacrificed in ceremonies
- Sheep = status and respect

SHEEP PRODUCTS:

Meat (Atsį'):
- Mutton (primary meat)
- Roasted, stewed, dried
- Shared with family and community
- Ceremonial offerings

Wool (Bighaa'):
- Used for weaving blankets and rugs
- Sold to trading posts
- Traded for other goods
- Women's economic resource

Hides:
- Used for clothing
- Used for moccasins
- Used for drums
- Used for ropes

Milk (Ooljéé'):
- Sheep milk (less common than goat milk)
- Made into cheese (rare)
- Given to lambs primarily

SHEEP HERDING ECONOMY:

- Entire families participated
- Children started herding at age 5-6
- Sheep provided food, clothing, and trade goods
- Surplus sheep sold or traded
- Sheep allowed Navajo to participate in regional trade


PART 3: WEAVING ECONOMY
================================================================================

Weaving became a major source of income for Navajo women.

WEAVING FOR TRADE (1800s):

- Navajo rugs and blankets highly valued
- Traded to other tribes
- Traded to Spanish and Mexicans
- Traded to Anglo-Americans
- Became Navajo's most famous export

TRADING POST ECONOMY:

- Trading posts bought rugs
- Paid in goods (not cash initially)
- Rugs valued by size, quality, design
- Women's economic independence
- Still important today

RUG PRICES (Historical):

- 1880s: $5-20 per rug
- 1900s: $10-100 per rug
- 1920s: $20-500 per rug
- 1950s: $50-1,000 per rug
- Today: $100-10,000+ per rug (some $100,000+)

FAMOUS WEAVING TRADING POSTS:

- Hubbell Trading Post (Ganado, AZ)
- Two Grey Hills Trading Post (NM)
- Crystal Trading Post (NM)
- Wide Ruins Trading Post (AZ)
- Teec Nos Pos Trading Post (AZ)

WEAVING TODAY:

- Still major source of income for women
- Some weavers earn $50,000+ annually
- High-end rugs sell for $10,000-100,000
- Rugs sold through trading posts, galleries, auctions
- Internet sales increasing


PART 4: SILVERSMITHING ECONOMY
================================================================================

Silversmithing became a major source of income for Navajo men.

INTRODUCTION OF SILVERSMITHING (1850s):

- Atsidi Sani (Old Smith) learned from Mexican smiths
- Navajo silver quickly became valued
- Silversmithing spread rapidly
- By 1880s, widespread

SILVERSMITHING FOR TRADE:

- Silver jewelry traded to other tribes
- Sold to trading posts
- Sold to tourists (after railroads arrived)
- Became famous worldwide

SILVER PRICES (Historical):

- 1880s: $1-5 per piece
- 1900s: $5-50 per piece
- 1920s: $10-100 per piece
- 1950s: $20-500 per piece
- Today: $50-5,000+ per piece (some $50,000+)

SILVERSMITHING TODAY:

- Still major source of income for men
- Some silversmiths earn $50,000+ annually
- Sold through trading posts, galleries, jewelry stores
- Internet sales increasing
- Some work for jewelry companies

TURQUOUSE (Dootł'izh):

- Most important stone in Navajo jewelry
- Sourced from New Mexico, Arizona, Nevada
- Valued by color (blue highest)
- Set in silver
- Prices vary widely ($10-$10,000+ per stone)


PART 5: TRADING POST ECONOMY (1800s-1900s)
================================================================================

Trading posts were the center of Navajo economic life.

HISTORY OF TRADING POSTS:

- First trading posts: 1850s (after U.S. occupation)
- Provided goods not available on reservation
- Traded wool, sheep, rugs, silver
- Became community centers
- Many operated by non-Navajo (some by Navajo)

THE TRADING POST SYSTEM:

Navajo brought:
- Wool
- Sheep
- Mutton
- Rugs
- Silver jewelry
- Pinon nuts
- Labor

Trading posts gave:
- Coffee
- Sugar
- Flour
- Cloth (velvet, calico)
- Tools
- Ammunition
- Tobacco
- Canned goods
- Dry goods

THE CREDIT SYSTEM:

- Trading posts extended credit
- Navajo bought on credit (to be paid later)
- Credit repaid with wool, sheep, rugs, silver
- Sometimes exploited (high interest)
- Some families trapped in debt

FAMOUS TRADING POSTS:

- Hubbell Trading Post (Ganado, AZ) - still operating
- Two Grey Hills Trading Post (NM) - still operating
- Crystal Trading Post (NM) - closed
- Wide Ruins Trading Post (AZ) - still operating
- Teec Nos Pos Trading Post (AZ) - still operating
- Shiprock Trading Post (NM) - still operating
- Kayenta Trading Post (AZ) - still operating

THE TRADER'S ROLE:

- Business person
- Banker (extended credit)
- Advisor
- Intermediary with outsiders
- Friend (sometimes)
- Exploiter (sometimes)
- Cultural broker

MODERN TRADING POSTS:

- Fewer trading posts than in the past
- Some still operate in remote areas
- Many replaced by convenience stores
- Some have become tourist destinations
- Hubbell Trading Post is National Historic Site
- Some owned by Navajo now


PART 6: THE LONG WALK AND ECONOMIC DESTRUCTION (1864-1868)
================================================================================

The Long Walk devastated the Navajo economy.

DESTRUCTION OF SHEEP:

- U.S. Army destroyed sheep (1863-1864)
- Thousands of sheep killed
- Primary wealth destroyed
- Economic collapse

DESTRUCTION OF FIELDS:

- Corn fields burned
- Orchards destroyed
- Food sources eliminated
- Agricultural economy destroyed

DESTRUCTION OF HOGANS:

- Hogans burned
- Homes destroyed
- Belongings lost
- Community infrastructure destroyed

AT BOSQUE REDONDO (Hwéeldi):

- No sheep (little grazing land)
- Poor farming (alkaline soil)
- Dependence on U.S. rations
- No economic activity
- Poverty and starvation

AFTER THE LONG WALK (1868):

- Returning with almost nothing
- U.S. government provided some sheep (15,000)
- Provided some farming equipment
- Provided seeds
- Rebuilding from zero


PART 7: REBUILDING THE ECONOMY (1868-1930)
================================================================================

After the Long Walk, the Navajo rebuilt their economy.

REBUILDING SHEEP HERDS:

- U.S. government provided 15,000 sheep (1868)
- Additional sheep purchased or traded
- Sheep herds grew rapidly
- By 1880s, sheep numbered 500,000+
- By 1900, sheep numbered 1,000,000+
- By 1930, sheep numbered 2,000,000+ (overgrazing)

REBUILDING WEAVING:

- Weaving continued after return
- Trading posts bought rugs
- New designs developed
- Regional styles emerged
- Rugs became major export

REBUILDING SILVERSMITHING:

- Silversmithing continued after return
- Trading posts bought jewelry
- New techniques developed
- Tourist market grew
- Jewelry became major export

RAILROAD ECONOMY (1880s):

- Railroads reached Navajo Nation
- Tourism increased
- Navajo jewelry and rugs sold to tourists
- New markets opened
- Economic growth

TRADING POST EXPANSION (1880-1930):

- Trading posts spread across Navajo Nation
- Most communities had a trading post
- Credit system widespread
- Barter economy (cash rare)
- Dependency on trading posts


PART 8: STOCK REDUCTION (1930-1940s)
================================================================================

The U.S. government forced Navajo to reduce sheep herds.

THE PROBLEM:

- Overgrazing (sheep too many for land)
- Erosion
- Dust storms
- Land degradation
- U.S. government concerned

THE SOLUTION (forced):

- Stock reduction program (1930s-1940s)
- Sheep herds reduced by 50-75%
- Goats and horses also reduced
- Not voluntary (forced)
- Inadequate compensation

THE IMPACT:

- Economic devastation (sheep = wealth)
- Poverty increased
- Hunger and malnutrition
- Resentment toward U.S. government
- Loss of economic independence
- Lasting trauma

The Navajo called this "The Frightening" (because of the fear and trauma).

ECONOMIC CONSEQUENCES:

- Sheep herds never fully recovered
- Many families never regained former wealth
- Forced to find other income sources
- Migration to cities for work
- Increased wage labor


PART 9: WAGE ECONOMY (1940s-Present)
================================================================================

Navajo increasingly work for wages rather than traditional economy.

SOURCES OF WAGE INCOME:

- Navajo Nation government (largest employer)
- Indian Health Service (IHS)
- Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA)
- Casinos (gaming)
- Tribal enterprises
- Off-reservation employment
- Federal and state governments

UNEMPLOYMENT:

- High unemployment (40-50% on reservation)
- Higher in remote areas
- Higher for youth
- Higher for those without education
- Some underemployment (part-time, low-wage)

MAJOR EMPLOYERS ON NAVAJO NATION:

- Navajo Nation government (5,000+ employees)
- Indian Health Service (3,000+ employees)
- BIA/Bureau of Indian Education (2,000+ employees)
- Casinos (2,000+ employees)
- Diné College (500+ employees)
- Navajo Tribal Utility Authority (500+ employees)
- Navajo Nation Shopping Centers (500+ employees)
- Hospitals and clinics (multiple)

COMMUTING:

- Many Navajos commute off-reservation for work
- Gallup, Farmington, Flagstaff, Albuquerque, Phoenix, Salt Lake City
- Long commutes (1-3 hours each way)
- Some live away from reservation (work in cities)
- Remittances sent to family on reservation

CASH ECONOMY:

- Cash increasingly replaces barter
- Credit cards and debit cards
- Banks on reservation (limited)
- Payday lenders (high interest)
- Poverty still widespread


PART 10: GAMING ECONOMY
================================================================================

Gaming (casinos) provides revenue for Navajo Nation.

HISTORY OF GAMING:

- Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (1988)
- Navajo Nation approved gaming (1990s)
- First casino opened (1990s)
- Multiple casinos now operating

NAVAJO NATION CASINOS:

- Fire Rock Navajo Casino (Church Rock, NM)
- Flowing Water Navajo Casino (near Shiprock, NM)
- Northern Edge Navajo Casino (near Farmington, NM)
- Twin Arrows Navajo Casino (near Flagstaff, AZ)
- Others in development

CASINO REVENUE:

- Millions of dollars annually
- Fund Navajo Nation government
- Fund scholarships
- Fund infrastructure
- Fund social programs

CONTROVERSIES:

- Some Navajos oppose gambling (traditional values)
- Addiction concerns
- Not all chapters benefit equally
- Revenue less than expected (in some cases)
- Competition from state-run lotteries, other tribes

CASINO EMPLOYMENT:

- 2,000+ jobs (estimate)
- Many Navajos employed
- Some management positions
- Entry-level jobs (dealers, cashiers, security)
- Training programs


PART 11: NATURAL RESOURCE ECONOMY
================================================================================

Navajo Nation has significant natural resources.

COAL:

- Major resource (Peabody Energy)
- Black Mesa (Arizona) - largest mine
- Shiprock (New Mexico) - smaller mines
- Coal slurry pipeline (controversial)
- Environmental concerns
- Decline (coal demand decreasing)

OIL AND GAS:

- Significant reserves
- Aneth Field (Utah) - major producer
- Other fields across Navajo Nation
- Royalties to Navajo Nation
- Environmental concerns

URANIUM:

- Significant reserves
- Mined from 1940s-1980s
- Health impacts (Navajo miners)
- Abandoned mines (cleanup ongoing)
- No current mining (moratorium)

RENEWABLE ENERGY:

- Solar potential (high)
- Wind potential (moderate)
- Some solar projects developed
- Wind projects in planning
- Transition from fossil fuels

NATURAL RESOURCE REVENUE:

- Millions of dollars annually
- Fund Navajo Nation government
- Fund scholarships
- Fund infrastructure
- Declining (coal, oil, gas demand decreasing)


PART 12: TOURISM ECONOMY
================================================================================

Tourism is a growing part of Navajo economy.

MAJOR TOURIST ATTRACTIONS:

- Monument Valley (Tsé Biiʼ Nidzisgai)
- Canyon de Chelly (Tséyi')
- Four Corners Monument
- Shiprock (Tsé Bit'a'í)
- Window Rock (Tségháhoodzání)
- Navajo National Monument
- Hubbell Trading Post
- Many other sites

TOURISM REVENUE:

- Millions of dollars annually
- Park entrance fees
- Guided tours (Navajo-owned)
- Gift shops (jewelry, rugs, crafts)
- Restaurants
- Hotels (limited)
- Campgrounds

TOURISM EMPLOYMENT:

- Tour guides (Navajo-owned businesses)
- Gift shop employees
- Restaurant workers
- Hotel workers
- Park rangers
- Artisans (selling to tourists)

CHALLENGES:

- Seasonality (peak summer, slow winter)
- Remote location (far from cities)
- Limited infrastructure (hotels, restaurants)
- COVID-19 impact (tourism declined)
- Competition (other attractions)


PART 13: MODERN ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT
================================================================================

Navajo Nation is working to diversify its economy.

TRIBAL ENTERPRISES:

- Navajo Nation Shopping Centers
- Navajo Nation Hospitality (hotels)
- Navajo Nation Gaming Enterprise
- Navajo Agricultural Products Industry (NAPI)
- Navajo Arts and Crafts Enterprise
- Navajo Nation Telecommunications

NAPI (Navajo Agricultural Products Industry):
- Large farming operation
- Located in Shiprock, NM
- Grows corn, wheat, alfalfa
- Major employer
- Millions in revenue

TELECOMMUNICATIONS:

- Navajo Nation Telecommunications
- Providing internet access (limited)
- Cell phone service (some areas)
- Broadband expansion
- Federal grants

SMALL BUSINESS DEVELOPMENT:

- Navajo Nation Division of Economic Development
- Small business loans
- Business training
- Technical assistance
- Some success, many challenges

ENTREPRENEURSHIP:

- Navajo-owned businesses (restaurants, stores, tour companies, art galleries, construction)
- Many small businesses
- Some large businesses (few)
- Challenges (access to capital, location, market size)

ECONOMIC CHALLENGES:

- High unemployment
- Limited infrastructure
- Remote location (far from markets)
- Small population (limited local market)
- Low educational attainment (some areas)
- Lack of capital
- Bureaucracy

ECONOMIC OPPORTUNITIES:

- Renewable energy (solar, wind)
- Tourism (growth potential)
- Telecommunications (broadband expansion)
- Small business development
- Arts and crafts (online sales)
- Value-added agriculture
- Healthcare (expanding IHS)


PART 14: ECONOMIC PHRASES
================================================================================

VOCABULARY:

- Na'nish - Work / job
- Naa'ní - He/she works
- Naa'nish - Work (noun)
- Béeso - Money
- Béeso bił - With money
- Béeso yá'át'ééh - Money is good
- Béeso doo yá'át'ééh da - Money is not good (traditional value)
- Na'nish bąą - Because of work
- Na'nish sin - I want work
- Dibé - Sheep (wealth)
- Bighaa' - Wool
- Atsį' - Meat

PHRASES:

- Na'nish nisin - I need work / I want work
- Na'nish nizhóní - The work is good
- Na'nish yá'át'ééh - The work is good
- Béeso shił nizhóní - Money is good to me
- Béeso shił yá'át'ééh - Money is good to me
- Dibé shił nizhóní - Sheep are good to me (I am wealthy in sheep)

ECONOMIC VALUES:

- Dibé baa nitsíjíkees - I am thinking of sheep (I care about my wealth)
- Béeso baa nitsíjíkees - I am thinking of money (I care about money)
- Doo béeso baa nitsíjíkees da - I don't care about money (traditional value)
- K'é baa nitsíjíkees - I am thinking of family/kinship (most important)

Sources: Navajo Nation Division of Economic Development, "Navajo Trading" (Willow Roberts Powers), "The Navajo Economy" (Navajo Nation), "The Long Walk" (Navajo Nation Historical Series).
