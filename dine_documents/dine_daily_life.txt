DINÉ DAILY LIFE: TRADITIONAL HOMES, CLOTHING, FOOD, AND DAILY ACTIVITIES
================================================================================
Sources: Navajo Nation Cultural Resources, "Navajo Life" (Clyde Kluckhohn), Museum of Northern Arizona


PART 1: THE HOGAN (HOOGHAN)
================================================================================

The hogan (hooghan) is the traditional Diné home.

WHAT IS A HOGAN?:
- A traditional Navajo dwelling
- Round or conical shape
- Made of wood, earth, and stone
- Facing east (toward the rising sun)
- Sacred and ceremonial space

THE TWO TYPES OF HOGANS:

1. FORKED STICK HOGAN (Tsé bee hooghan):
   - Most traditional style
   - Three forked poles form the frame
   - Covered with bark, brush, and earth
   - Used for ceremonies
   - Considered the original hogan

2. LOG HOGAN (Kin bee hooghan):
   - Built with stacked logs
   - Mortar (mud) between logs
   - More permanent
   - Common for daily living
   - Developed after Spanish contact

HOGAN CONSTRUCTION:

Step 1 - Choosing the location:
   - Not in the path of flash floods
   - Near water source
   - Protected from wind
   - East-facing slope preferred
   - Not too close to other hogans

Step 2 - Gathering materials:
   - Logs (cedar or pine preferred)
   - Bark and brush
   - Earth and mud
   - Stones for the base

Step 3 - Building the frame:
   - Three or more forked poles joined at top
   - Space between poles for smoke hole
   - Main poles oriented to the four directions
   - East-facing door

Step 4 - Adding walls:
   - Logs stacked horizontally (log hogan)
   - Brush and earth for smaller hogans
   - Mud chinking between logs
   - Door on the east side

Step 5 - The roof:
   - Smaller poles across the frame
   - Bark or brush layer
   - Earth covering
   - Smoke hole at top (for fire inside)

HOGAN LAYOUT (INTERIOR):

Entering the hogan:
   - Always enter moving clockwise
   - Door faces east
   - Men sit on north side
   - Women sit on south side

The center:
   - Fire pit (replaces stove today)
   - Smoke hole above
   - Sacred space
   - Where ceremonies happen

North side (men's area):
   - Men's tools and belongings
   - Hunting equipment
   - Ceremonial objects
   - Men sit here

South side (women's area):
   - Cooking area
   - Weaving loom
   - Women's belongings
   - Women sit here

West side (back):
   - Sleeping area
   - Storage
   - Most private area

PROPER HOGAN ETIQUETTE:

- Always enter moving clockwise
- Never walk between people and the fire
- Sit where directed by host
- Men on north side, women on south side
- Do not step over people
- Do not touch ceremonial objects
- Remove hats inside
- Speak quietly

THE HOGAN IS SACRED:

- The hogan represents the universe
- The round shape represents the sun
- The four main poles represent the four directions
- The door facing east greets the rising sun
- The smoke hole connects earth and sky
- The hogan is a living being

MODERN HOGANS:

- Many Navajos still live in hogans
- Some hogans have electricity and plumbing
- Traditional shape maintained
- Modern materials (metal roofs, plywood)
- Hogans are still built for ceremonies
- Many families have both a traditional hogan and a modern house


PART 2: TRADITIONAL CLOTHING (ÉÉ' DOO YISTŁ'INÍGÍÍ)
================================================================================

Traditional Diné clothing was practical and beautiful.

WOMEN'S CLOTHING (Before Spanish contact):

- Skirt (wrapped around waist)
- Blanket or shawl (upper body)
- Moccasins (high-top)
- Jewelry (shell, turquoise)
- Hair in Tsiiyéél (bun)

WOMEN'S CLOTHING (After Spanish contact - influenced by Spanish/Mexican styles):

- Velvet blouse (bright colors)
- Full, pleated skirt
- Sash (concho belt)
- Moccasins (knee-high)
- Tsiiyéél (hair bun)
- Jewelry (silver and turquoise)

Men's clothing (Before Spanish contact):
- Breechcloth
- Blanket (wrapped around body)
- Moccasins (knee-high)
- Hair (long, sometimes tied)

MEN'S CLOTHING (After Spanish contact):
- Velvet shirt (bright colors)
- Pants (calico or denim)
- Concho belt (silver)
- Headband (cloth)
- Moccasins (ankle-high)
- Hair (long, often worn loose)

CLOTHING MATERIALS:

- Velvet (introduced by Spanish, adopted by Navajo)
- Calico (traded cloth)
- Wool (from sheep, for blankets)
- Cotton (traded, rare)
- Leather (deer, elk, cow)
- Sinew (for sewing)

Blankets and shawls:
- Woven by women
- Used for warmth and modesty
- Patterns indicated status
- Some were ceremonial
- Traded for other goods

JEWELRY:

- Necklaces (shell, turquoise, silver)
- Earrings (silver, turquoise)
- Bracelets (silver, stamped)
- Rings (silver, turquoise)
- Concho belts (silver discs on leather)

Turquoise:
- Most sacred stone
- Protects the wearer
- Represents the sky and water
- Worn daily (not just for special occasions)

MODERN CLOTHING:

- Most Navajos wear Western clothing daily
- Traditional clothing for ceremonies, powwows, and special events
- Some wear traditional jewelry daily
- Moccasins are still worn (especially at ceremonies)
- Tsiiyéél (hair bun) is still worn by women (traditional and modern)


PART 3: TRADITIONAL FOODS (CH'IYÁÁN)
================================================================================

Traditional Diné foods were based on agriculture, hunting, and gathering.

THE THREE SISTERS (Staple crops):

1. CORN (Naadą́ą́'):
   - Most important food
   - Many varieties (white, blue, yellow, red, black)
   - Eaten fresh, dried, or ground into flour
   - Used in ceremonies

2. BEANS (Naa'oh):
   - Dried beans stored for winter
   - Protein source
   - Eaten with corn

3. SQUASH (Ch'ééh):
   - Summer squash (eaten fresh)
   - Winter squash (stored)
   - Seeds roasted for snacks

Other traditional foods:

MEAT:
- Mutton (sheep meat) - most common meat
- Deer (hunted)
- Rabbit (hunted)
- Prairie dog (traditional)
- Goat (less common)
- Horse (rare, only in hard times)

GATHERED FOODS:
- Wild greens (spring)
- Cactus fruits (prickly pear)
- Berries (summer)
- Nuts (pinon - fall)
- Seeds (various)
- Wild onions
- Yucca fruit

PINON NUTS:
- Most important gathered food
- Harvested in fall (October-November)
- Eaten raw or roasted
- Stored for winter
- Also used for trading

BREAD:
- Cornbread (made from blue cornmeal)
- Frybread (post-contact, not traditional)
- Ash bread (buried in hot ashes)

DRINKS:
- Water (tó)
- Corn pollen tea
- Mint tea (wild mint)
- Coffee (post-contact)
- Soda (modern)

HOW FOOD WAS PREPARED:

CORN PREPARATION:
1. Corn dried on the cob
2. Kernels removed
3. Ground into flour (metate and mano - grinding stone)
4. Made into bread, mush, or stew

Cooking methods:
- Boiling (clay pots)
- Roasting (over fire)
- Baking (in earth ovens)
- Grinding (for flour)

Food storage:
- Dried meat (jerky)
- Dried corn
- Dried beans
- Pinon nuts stored in shell
- Root cellar (underground storage)

MEALS:

Breakfast (Awéé'):
- Corn mush or bread
- Coffee (post-contact)

Lunch (Awéé'):
- Leftovers
- Bread and mutton

Dinner (E'elyá):
- Main meal
- Mutton stew with corn
- Frybread (modern)
- Coffee or tea

Ceremonial foods:
- Corn pollen (sacred)
- Blue corn mush (ceremonial)
- Mutton (offered to Holy People)
- Bread (offered)


PART 4: SHEEP HERDING (DIBÉ NA'ALZHO')
================================================================================

Sheep have been central to Diné life since the 1600s.

HISTORY OF SHEEP:

- Spanish brought sheep to the Southwest (1600s)
- Navajo adopted sheep herding quickly
- Sheep became the center of Navajo economy and culture
- The Long Walk (1864-1868) destroyed most sheep
- After 1868, sheep herds were rebuilt
- Today, sheep are still important

THE IMPORTANCE OF SHEEP:

- Food (mutton, milk)
- Clothing (wool for weaving)
- Trade (sheep, wool, mutton)
- Ceremonies (sheep sacrificed)
- Wealth (sheep = wealth)

A man's wealth was measured in sheep:
- Poor: 0-50 sheep
- Average: 50-200 sheep
- Rich: 200-1000+ sheep

HERDING TODAY:

- Fewer Navajos herd sheep than in the past
- Grazing permits required on Navajo Nation
- Some families maintain large herds
- Wool is sold or used for weaving
- Mutton is still a staple food

SHEEP VOCABULARY:

- Dibé - Sheep
- Dibé na'azí - Herd of sheep
- Dibé yázhí - Lamb
- Bighaa' - Wool
- Atsį' - Meat
- Ooljéé' - Milk


PART 5: DAILY CHORES AND ACTIVITIES
================================================================================

Traditional Diné daily life followed a rhythm.

DAILY ROUTINE:

Morning (Awéé' - Dawn to mid-morning):
- Wake at dawn
- Greet the sun
- Feed and water sheep
- Milk sheep (if applicable)
- Bring sheep to grazing
- Breakfast
- Begin daily chores

Mid-day (Ałní'íní - Late morning to mid-afternoon):
- Main work period
- Weaving (women)
- Silversmithing (men)
- Farming (both)
- Household chores
- Lunch (light meal)

Afternoon (Iíłní' - Mid-afternoon to sunset):
- Bring sheep back from grazing
- Water sheep
- Evening chores
- Dinner preparation
- Dinner

Evening (Tł'éé' - Sunset to bedtime):
- Eat dinner
- Family time
- Stories (winter only)
- Bed (soon after dark)

SEASONAL ACTIVITIES:

Spring:
- Planting corn, beans, squash
- Shearing sheep
- Lambing season
- Gathering wild greens
- Cleaning hogans

Summer:
- Weeding crops
- Herding sheep
- Gathering berries
- Trading at summer trading posts
- Ceremonies (Blessing Way)

Fall:
- Harvesting corn, beans, squash
- Gathering pinon nuts
- Fall shearing (some sheep)
- Preparing for winter
- Hunting deer

Winter:
- Telling winter stories (Hááne'é)
- Weaving (indoors)
- Silversmithing (indoors)
- Eating stored food
- Ceremonies (Night Way)
- Staying warm by fire

GENDER ROLES:

Women's work:
- Weaving
- Cooking
- Child care
- Farming (some tasks)
- Gathering
- Household chores
- Mutton processing

Men's work:
- Silversmithing
- Hunting
- Heavy farming tasks
- Building hogans
- Herding (both)
- Trading
- Ceremonies (as Hataałii)

Children's work:
- Herding sheep (starting at age 5-6)
- Gathering wood
- Fetching water
- Child care (older children)
- Helping with chores
- Learning by observing

CEREMONIAL TOOLS:

PART 7: THE TRADING POST (TÓ NEEL'ĮĮD)
================================================================================

Trading posts were centers of Diné economic life.

HISTORY OF TRADING POSTS:

- First trading posts: 1850s
- Provided goods not available on the reservation
- Traded wool, sheep, rugs, and silver
- Became community centers
- Many still operate today

WHAT WAS TRADED:

Navajo gave:
- Wool
- Sheep
- Mutton
- Rugs
- Silver jewelry
- Pinon nuts
- Work (as employees)

Navajo received:
- Coffee
- Sugar
- Flour
- Cloth (velvet, calico)
- Tools
- Ammunition
- Tobacco
- Canned goods

THE TRADER'S ROLE:

- Business person
- Banker (extended credit)
- Advisor
- Intermediary with outsiders
- Friend (sometimes)
- Exploiter (sometimes)

FAMOUS TRADING POSTS:

- Hubbell Trading Post (Ganado, AZ - still operating)
- Two Grey Hills Trading Post (NM)
- Crystal Trading Post (NM)
- Wide Ruins Trading Post (AZ)
- Teec Nos Pos Trading Post (AZ)

MODERN TRADING POSTS:

- Fewer trading posts than in the past
- Some still operate in remote areas
- Many have been replaced by convenience stores
- Some have become tourist destinations
- Hubbell Trading Post is a National Historic Site


PART 8: TRANSPORTATION
================================================================================

TRADITIONAL TRANSPORTATION:

- Walking (most common)
- Horse (after Spanish contact)
- Burro (pack animal)
- Wagon (post-contact)

HORSES:
- Introduced by Spanish (1600s)
- Navajo became skilled riders
- Horses used for:
   - Herding sheep
   - Travel
   - Hunting
   - Trading
   - Warfare

MODERN TRANSPORTATION:

- Automobiles (most common)
- Trucks (for herding and hauling)
- SUVs (for rough roads)
- Navajo Nation has limited public transportation
- Some remote areas still require horses or ATVs
- Air travel (small airports in major towns)

CHALLENGES:

- Navajo Nation is large (27,000+ square miles)
- Many roads are unpaved
- Some areas inaccessible in bad weather
- Long distances to services
- Few public transportation options

PART 10: MODERN DAILY LIFE
================================================================================

Today, Diné daily life blends traditional and modern.

TYPICAL MODERN DAY:

Morning:
- Wake up (alarm clock)
- Breakfast (cereal, coffee, sometimes traditional)
- Children go to school (bus or car)
- Adults go to work (off-reservation or tribal employment)
- Elders may herd sheep or do traditional activities

Mid-day:
- Work or school
- Lunch (sandwich, leftovers, fast food)
- Some families eat traditional foods

Afternoon:
- Children return from school
- Homework
- After-school activities (sports, clubs)
- Some families herd sheep after work
- Shopping (grocery store, convenience store)

Evening:
- Dinner (often traditional mutton stew or modern foods)
- Family time
- Television or internet
- Homework
- Bed

CHALLENGES OF MODERN LIFE:

- Unemployment (high on Navajo Nation)
- Poverty (many families)
- Lack of infrastructure (water, electricity, internet in some areas)
- Long commutes to work
- Cost of living (higher than off-reservation)
- Health issues (diabetes, heart disease)
- Language loss (fewer young people speak Navajo)

TRADITIONAL ACTIVITIES THAT CONTINUE:

- Sheep herding (some families)
- Weaving (many women)
- Silversmithing (many men)
- Ceremonies (thousands each year)
- Storytelling (winter months)
- Traditional foods (especially mutton, corn)
- Clan introductions (still practiced)
- Tsiiyéél (hair bun - still worn by some women)


PART 11: DINÉ DAILY LIFE QUOTES
================================================================================

Elders' wisdom about daily life:

"Wake with the sun. Greet the dawn. This is how we begin in beauty." - Diné elder

"The hogan faces east because the sun brings life. Every morning, we greet the sun. Every evening, we thank it." - Traditional teaching

"Sheep are our life. They feed us, clothe us, and give us wealth. Without sheep, we are poor." - Traditional saying

"Work is not punishment. Work is life. When we work, we are alive. When we stop working, we begin to die." - Diné elder

"The old ways are not gone. They are sleeping. They wait for us to wake them." - Diné elder

"The hogan is not a house. It is a mother. It holds us, protects us, and teaches us." - Traditional teaching
