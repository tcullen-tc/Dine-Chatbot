DINÉ HISTORY & HEROES: THE LONG WALK, TREATY OF 1868, CODE TALKERS, AND LEADERS
================================================================================
Sources: Navajo Nation Historical Archives, Library of Congress, National Museum of the American Indian


PART 1: THE LONG WALK (HWÉELDI - 1864-1868)
================================================================================

The Long Walk (Hwéeldi) was the forced relocation of the Diné people by the United States Army.

WHAT HAPPENED:

In 1863-1864, under the command of Colonel Kit Carson, the U.S. Army:
- Burned Navajo homes, fields, and orchards
- Destroyed livestock (sheep, horses, cattle)
- Captured Navajo people and forced them to surrender
- Approximately 8,000-10,000 Diné were forced to walk

THE MARCH:

Distance: Over 300 miles (from Navajo homeland to Fort Sumner, New Mexico)
Route: Through Arizona and New Mexico territory
Duration: Approximately 18 days
Conditions: Harsh winter weather, little food or water, no proper clothing

Deaths during the march:
- Hundreds died from exposure, starvation, and disease
- Elderly, children, and sick suffered most
- Bodies were left along the trail

AT BOSQUE REDONDO (HWÉELDI):

What was supposed to be a reservation:
- 40 square miles of barren land at Bosque Redondo (Fort Sumner)
- Alkaline soil made farming impossible
- The land was also home to Mescalero Apache (forced there as well)
- Conflict between tribes was encouraged by the Army

Conditions at Hwéeldi:
- Inadequate food rations (often spoiled or insufficient)
- Contaminated water from the Pecos River
- Disease: dysentery, smallpox, malaria, tuberculosis
- Poor shelter (hovels made of sticks and mud)
- Attempts to escape were punished

Deaths at Hwéeldi:
- Approximately 2,000 Diné died during the four years (1864-1868)
- One-third of babies born died in infancy
- Many elderly and sick perished

LIFE AT HWÉELDI:

Daily existence:
- Ration lines for spoiled flour and rancid bacon
- Constant illness and hunger
- Forced labor details
- Children taken to attend English schools
- Attempts to force Diné to adopt European farming methods

Resistance:
- Diné maintained their language and ceremonies in secret
- Traditional leaders kept the people united
- Escape attempts, though many were caught and returned
- Prayer and spiritual practices continued


PART 2: THE TREATY OF 1868
================================================================================

The Treaty of 1868 ended the Long Walk and established the Navajo Reservation.

NEGOTIATIONS:

Date: June 1, 1868
Location: Fort Sumner, New Mexico (Hwéeldi)
Navajo leaders: Barboncito, Manuelito, Ganado Mucho, and others
U.S. representatives: General William T. Sherman and Colonel Samuel Tappan

Barboncito's speech to General Sherman:
"The bringing us here has caused suffering. Our women have been taken away, our children have been taken away... We have been confined here for four years and our numbers have decreased. The people are dying. We want to go home."

KEY TERMS OF THE TREATY:

Article I: Peace between the United States and the Navajo Nation
Article II: Navajo Nation to return to their homeland
Article III: Reservation boundaries established
   - 3.5 million acres (later expanded)
   - Within Arizona, New Mexico, and Utah
   - Land was considered "wasteland" by the government

Article IV: U.S. government to provide:
   - $150,000 for buildings, farming equipment, and seeds
   - 15,000 sheep and goats
   - 500 cattle
   - 500,000 pounds of flour
   - Clothing and other goods

Article V: Schools and teachers
   - U.S. to provide a schoolhouse and teacher for every 30 children
   - This became the boarding school system (which later caused harm)

Article VI: Navajo sovereignty
   - Recognized Navajo Nation as a separate government
   - Reservation not to be entered without Navajo permission
   - Navajo laws to be respected

Article VII: Navajo agree not to make war on the United States

THE RETURN HOME (1868):

June 1868: Treaty signed
July 1868: Approximately 7,000 Diné began the journey home
The return march: 300 miles back to their homeland
Reaction upon return:
   - Homes, fields, orchards were destroyed
   - Livestock was gone
   - Sacred places had been disturbed
   - The people had to rebuild from nothing

Barboncito's words upon returning:
"I see the homeland. I see the sacred mountains. I see where the Holy People placed us. We are home."


PART 3: CHIEF BARBONCITO (1820-1871)
================================================================================

Barboncito (Hastiin Hastiin - "The One Who Speaks for the People") was a primary Navajo leader during the Long Walk.

Role in Navajo history:
- Principal Navajo negotiator at the Treaty of 1868
- Spoke for all Navajo people during the negotiations
- Led the Diné back to their homeland
- Worked to preserve Navajo culture during and after the Long Walk

Key achievements:
- Convinced General Sherman to allow the Navajo to return home
- Negotiated for larger reservation boundaries
- Secured livestock and supplies for the people
- Maintained Navajo unity during the crisis

Legacy:
- Remembered as the greatest Navajo treaty negotiator
- His words at the treaty are still quoted today
- Statues and memorials honor his leadership
- His descendants continue as Navajo leaders


PART 4: CHIEF MANUELITO (1818-1893)
================================================================================

Manuelito (Hastiin Ch'ilhaajinii - "The One Who Grows from Darkness") was a fierce warrior and leader.

Role in Navajo history:
- Led resistance against the U.S. Army before and during the Long Walk
- Never surrendered - escaped capture multiple times
- Signed the Treaty of 1868 after the return
- Became head of Navajo Police after the treaty
- Advocated for Navajo education while preserving culture

Key achievements:
- Defended Navajo lands from encroachment
- Kept Navajo resistance alive during the Long Walk
- Worked with Barboncito to negotiate the treaty
- Built schools on the reservation (including Fort Defiance)
- Encouraged economic development through sheep herding

Famous quote:
"We were taken away from our land. We were kept like animals. We want to go home."

Legacy:
- Manuelito, New Mexico is named for him
- His leadership inspired later generations
- Statues honor him on the Navajo Nation
- He represents Navajo resilience and strength


PART 5: CHIEF GANADO MUCHO (1820-1893)
================================================================================

Ganado Mucho (Tó Bájísh Chíní - "Born for Water") was a key leader in rebuilding after the Long Walk.

Role in Navajo history:
- Signed the Treaty of 1868
- Focused on economic recovery after the return
- Built large sheep herds that became the foundation of Navajo economy
- Encouraged farming and trade

Key achievements:
- Helped re-establish traditional Navajo governance
- Negotiated for expanded reservation boundaries
- Built trading relationships with non-Navajo merchants
- His sheep herds supplied wool for Navajo weavers

Legacy:
- Ganado, Arizona is named for him
- His economic vision helped the Navajo recover from the Long Walk
- Remembered as a peacemaker and builder


PART 6: THE NAVAJO CODE TALKERS (1942-1945)
================================================================================

The Navajo Code Talkers were Navajo Marines who developed an unbreakable code based on the Navajo language.

WHAT WAS THE CODE:

The Code Talkers created a code that:
- Used Navajo words for military terms
- Had two layers of encryption
- Was never broken by the enemy
- Was transmitted by voice (not written)

How the code worked:
- Military terms were given Navajo names
   Example: "turtle" = tank
   Example: "hummingbird" = fighter plane
   Example: "whale" = battleship

- Letters were represented by Navajo words
   A = "wol-la-chee" (ant)
   B = "shush" (bear)
   C = "moasi" (cat)
   (and so on)

- The code used 411 terms in the original version
- Expanded to over 600 terms by war's end
- Code Talkers memorized everything - nothing written down

WHY THE CODE WAS UNBREAKABLE:

1. Navajo was an unwritten language with no published grammar
2. Complex syntax and tonal qualities impossible for non-speakers
3. Code within a code (military terms + letter substitution)
4. All code was memorized - no written records
5. Code Talkers could encode, transmit, and decode in seconds

WHO WERE THE CODE TALKERS:

Original 29 Code Talkers (developed the code in 1942):
- Recruited from Navajo Nation
- Developed the code at Camp Pendleton, California
- Trained other Navajo Marines
- First to use the code in combat

Total Code Talkers:
- Over 400 Navajo served as Code Talkers during WWII
- Many also served in Korea and Vietnam
- All were Marines

Famous Code Talkers:
- Chester Nez (last of the original 29, died 2014)
- Samuel Tso
- Peter MacDonald
- Joe Hosteen Kellwood
- Carl Gorman (father of R.C. Gorman)

THEIR SERVICE:

Battles where Code Talkers served:
- Guadalcanal (1942)
- Tarawa (1943)
- Saipan (1944)
- Iwo Jima (1945) - famous flag raising
- Okinawa (1945)
- Many others

Effectiveness:
- The Japanese never broke the code
- Code Talkers saved countless American lives
- They were so effective that the code was classified until 1968
- The same code principles were used in Korea and Vietnam


PART 7: AFTER THE WAR - THE CODE TALKERS' SECRET
================================================================================

The Code Talkers could not tell their story for 23 years.

Classified secret:
- The code remained classified after WWII
- Code Talkers were sworn to secrecy
- They could not tell their families or friends what they did
- Many were asked about their service and could not answer
- Some were accused of being cowards because they couldn't explain

Declassification:
- 1968: The code was finally declassified
- Code Talkers could finally tell their story
- Many had kept the secret for decades
- Recognition came very late for some who had already passed away

Recognition:
- 1982: August 14 named National Navajo Code Talkers Day
- 2001: Code Talkers received Congressional Gold Medals
- 2008: Code Talkers Recognition Act (honors all tribes who served as Code Talkers)
- 2014: The last of the original 29 (Chester Nez) died

Chester Nez's words:
"I'm proud to be a Navajo. I'm proud to have served my country. We did something special."


PART 8: OTHER NOTABLE DINÉ HEROES
================================================================================

HOWARD GORMAN (1923-2022):
- World War II veteran (before becoming a Code Talker)
- Navajo Code Talker
- Father of famous artist R.C. Gorman
- Worked as a silversmith and teacher
- Activist for Navajo veterans' rights

PETER MACDONALD (born 1928):
- Navajo Code Talker (joined at age 16)
- Served in WWII and Korea
- Chairman of the Navajo Nation (1970-1982, 1987-1989)
- Advocated for Navajo economic development
- Worked to preserve Navajo language

ANNIE DODGE WAUNEKA (1910-1997):
- First woman elected to Navajo Tribal Council (1951)
- Advocate for Navajo education and healthcare
- Fought for voting rights for Navajo people
- Worked to preserve Navajo culture and language

PETERSON ZAH (born 1937):
- Chairman of the Navajo Nation (1983-1987, 1991-1995)
- President of the Navajo Nation (1995-1999)
- First Navajo to serve as Chairman under the new government system
- Advocate for Navajo education and health
- Worked to improve Navajo-U.S. relations

ALBERT LELAND (born 1965):
- Navajo educator and activist
- Former Vice President of the Navajo Nation (1999-2003)
- Advocate for Navajo language preservation
- Worked to improve Navajo schools
- Promoted Diné traditional teachings in education


PART 9: THE NAVAJO NATION TODAY
================================================================================

The Navajo Nation (Naabeehó Bináhásdzo) is the largest Native American reservation in the United States.

BASIC FACTS:
- Population: Over 250,000 enrolled members
- Land area: 27,000+ square miles
- Location: Arizona, New Mexico, Utah
- Capital: Window Rock, Arizona (Tségháhoodzání)

GOVERNMENT:
- Three branches: Executive, Legislative, Judicial
- President elected by Navajo voters
- 24-member Navajo Nation Council
- District courts and Supreme Court

ECONOMY:
- Coal, oil, and natural gas resources
- Tourism (Monument Valley, Canyon de Chelly, Four Corners)
- Navajo Arts and Crafts Enterprise
- Gaming (four casinos)
- Agriculture (sheep, cattle, farming)

CHALLENGES:
- High unemployment rate
- Lack of clean water in many areas
- Infrastructure needs (roads, electricity, internet)
- Language loss (younger speakers declining)
- Housing shortage

STRENGTHS:
- Strong cultural identity and traditions
- Sovereign nation status
- Growing economy in some sectors
- Youth movement to preserve language and culture
- Increasing educational attainment


PART 10: HONORING THE HEROES
================================================================================

Ways the Navajo Nation honors its heroes:

MEMORIALS AND MONUMENTS:
- Code Talker Memorial (Window Rock, AZ)
- Navajo Veterans Memorial Park (Window Rock, AZ)
- Long Walk memorials (Fort Sumner, NM; various locations)
- Statues of Barboncito, Manuelito, and other leaders

HOLIDAYS AND CELEBRATIONS:
- Navajo Nation Day (April 8 - Treaty of 1868 anniversary)
- National Navajo Code Talkers Day (August 14)
- Veterans Day (honors all Navajo veterans)
- Long Walk commemorations (February)

EDUCATION:
- Navajo Nation schools teach Diné history
- Code Talker curriculum in schools
- Oral history projects recording veterans' stories
- Scholarships for Navajo students

FAMILY TRADITIONS:
- Families tell stories of ancestors who survived the Long Walk
- Code Talker families share their relatives' experiences
- Veterans are honored in ceremonies and family gatherings
- Traditional songs remember the heroes

THE LASTING LEGACY:

The heroes of Diné history teach us:
- Resilience in the face of oppression
- The power of unity and cultural identity
- The importance of preserving language
- That one person can make a difference
- That the Diné will always endure

As the Navajo Nation flag says: "Naabeehó Bináhásdzo" - The Navajo Nation will survive.
