DINÉ BIZAAD - THE NAVAJO LANGUAGE: COMPREHENSIVE GUIDE

Sources: Navajo Nation Division of Education, Diné College, Navajo Language Academy, "The Navajo Language" (Young & Morgan)


PART 1: OVERVIEW OF DINÉ BIZAAD
================================================================================

Diné Bizaad (Navajo language) is the most widely spoken Native American language in the United States.

BASIC FACTS:

Language family: Athabaskan (Na-Dené)
Related languages: Apache (all dialects), Tłı̨chǫ (Dogrib), Dene Sųłiné (Chipewyan)
Number of speakers: Approximately 148,530 (as of 2021)
Language vitality: Endangered (UNESCO classification)
Geographic area: Navajo Nation (Arizona, New Mexico, Utah) and urban areas

UNIQUE FEATURES:

Why Navajo is considered difficult for English speakers:

1. Verb-heavy language - most meaning is in verbs, not nouns
2. Four tones (high, low, rising, falling)
3. Complex verb system with up to 500 prefixes
4. Word order: Subject-Object-Verb (English is Subject-Verb-Object)
5. No adjectives - descriptive words are verbs
6. Animacy hierarchy - verbs change based on how "alive" the subject is
7. Classificatory verbs - different verbs for handling different types of objects

EXAMPLE OF VERB COMPLEXITY:

Single English sentence: "I am going"
Navajo has multiple forms depending on context:
- Naashá - I am going (on foot, now)
- Yishááł - I am going (on foot, future)
- Neesháázh - I went (on foot, past)
- Deeshááł - I am about to go (on foot)
- Náshtįįh - I am going (riding a horse)
- Yishtįįh - I am going (riding a horse, future)
- Níyá - I arrived (having gone)
- Dziih - I am going (in a vehicle)
- Yidziih - I am going (in a vehicle, future)

CLASSIFICATORY VERBS:

Navajo has different verbs for handling different types of objects:

Handling objects:
- -'ą́ - to handle a solid roundish object (ball, apple)
- -tłéé' - to handle a mushy object (mud, mashed potatoes)
- -tsoos - to handle a flat flexible object (paper, blanket)
- -tłéézh - to handle a slender flexible object (rope, snake)
- -jol - to handle a bulky object (sack of flour, backpack)
- -nil - to handle several objects (keys, coins)
- -yįįh - to handle a living object (animal, baby)
- -zhin - to handle a liquid container (bottle, cup)

Example: "I give it to you" changes based on the object:
- Na'niłtįįh - I give you a book (flat flexible)
- Na'ní'ą́ - I give you an apple (round)
- Na'níłtéézh - I give you a rope (slender flexible)
- Na'níłtéé' - I give you mud (mushy)


PART 2: THE NAVAJO ALPHABET AND PRONUNCIATION
================================================================================

Navajo uses a modified Latin alphabet with 36 letters.

CONSONANTS:

Navajo has many sounds not found in English:

Voiceless stops (similar to English but unaspirated):
- b [p] - like "spit" (no puff of air)
- d [t] - like "stick" (no puff of air)
- g [k] - like "skill" (no puff of air)
- ' [ʔ] - glottal stop (like "uh-oh")

Aspirated stops (with a puff of air):
- t [tʰ] - like "top"
- k [kʰ] - like "kite"
- ch [tʃʰ] - like "chin"

Ejective stops (made with glottal closure):
- t' [t'] - no English equivalent
- k' [k'] - no English equivalent
- ch' [tʃ'] - no English equivalent
- ts' [ts'] - no English equivalent

Fricatives:
- s [s] - like "see"
- z [z] - like "zoo"
- sh [ʃ] - like "ship"
- zh [ʒ] - like "measure"
- h [h] - like "hat"
- ł [ɬ] - voiceless L (no English equivalent)
- l [l] - like "light"

Nasals:
- m [m] - like "mother"
- n [n] - like "no"
- ng [ŋ] - like "sing" (only at ends of words)

Glides:
- w [w] - like "water"
- y [j] - like "yes"

VOWELS:

Navajo has 4 basic vowels, each with 4 variations (short, long, nasalized, and long nasalized):

a [ɑ] - like "father"
- á (high tone) - aá (long)
- ą (nasal) - aą (long nasal)

e [e] - like "pay"
- é (high tone) - eé (long)
- ę (nasal) - eę (long nasal)

i [ɪ] - like "pit"
- í (high tone) - ií (long)
- į (nasal) - iį (long nasal)

o [o] - like "go"
- ó (high tone) - oó (long)
- ǫ (nasal) - oǫ (long nasal)

TONES:

Navajo has four tones (pitch patterns):

1. High tone (á, é, í, ó) - marked with acute accent
2. Low tone (a, e, i, o) - unmarked
3. Rising tone (aá, eé, ií, oó) - low to high
4. Falling tone (áa, ée, íi, óo) - high to low

Tone changes meaning:
- aghaa' (hair) vs. agháá' (winter)
- bįįh (deer) vs. bííh (drought)
- tązh (tooth) vs. tázh (spotted)
- béésh (knife) vs. beesh (metal)

STRESS:

Stress is usually on the first syllable of a word:
- Shí-má (my mother)
- Ní-ma (your mother)
- Bí-má (his/her mother)


PART 3: NAVAJO GRAMMAR BASICS
================================================================================

WORD ORDER:

Standard word order: Subject - Object - Verb (SOV)

Example:
"John ałtį́į́' yiłchozh" - John coffee drinks (John drinks coffee)
- John (subject)
- ałtį́į́' (coffee - object)
- yiłchozh (drinks - verb)

"Shí naaltsoos nish'į́" - I book see (I see the book)
- Shí (I - subject)
- naaltsoos (book - object)
- nish'į́ (see - verb)

NOUNS:

Nouns do not change for plural (context indicates number):
- tł'ízí - goat / goats
- dibé - sheep / sheep
- łééchąą'í - dog / dogs

Possession is shown with prefixes:
- shi- (my) + má (mother) = shimá (my mother)
- ni- (your) + má (mother) = nimá (your mother)
- bi- (his/her) + má (mother) = bimá (his/her mother)
- nihi- (our) + má (mother) = nihimá (our mother)
- danihi- (your pl.) + má = danihimá (your [plural] mother)
- bí- (their) + má = bímá (their mother)

VERBS:

Verbs are the most complex part of Navajo. They consist of:

Prefix + prefix + ... + root

Example: yishááł (I am going)
- y- (3rd person object prefix)
- i- (thematic prefix)
- sh- (1st person subject prefix)
- ááł (verb root meaning "to go on foot")

Common verb modes:
- Imperfective (ongoing action): naashá - I am going
- Perfective (completed action): neesháázh - I went
- Future (will happen): deeshááł - I will go
- Usitative (habitual action): nishááh - I go (regularly)
- Optative (desired action): nááshá - I would go
- Progressive (continuous): náníshááh - I am going along

PRONOUNS:

Independent pronouns:
- Shí - I, me
- Ní - you
- Bí - he, she, him, her
- Nihí - we, us (excluding you)
- Danihí - we, us (including you)
- Daabí - they, them

Subject pronouns (incorporated into verb):
- sh- / -s- - I
- ni- / -n- - you
- bi- / -∅- - he/she
- nihi- / -n- - we (excl.)
- danihi- / -n- - we (incl.)
- daabi- / -∅- - they

QUESTION WORDS:

- Haash - what
- Háí - who
- Hádą́ - when
- Háadi - where
- Haashíí - how
- Haa - which
- Haashę́ę - why
- Hánít'éego - how many

QUESTION FORMATION:

Add question particle "shı̨́ı̨́" at the end of sentence:
- Ní yá'át'ééh shı̨́ı̨́? - Are you good? (How are you?)
- Naaltsoos yíníyą́ shı̨́ı̨́? - Did you see the book?

Or use question words:
- Haash yinílyé? - What is your name?
- Háadi nee'? - Where do you live?
- Haashíí aníná? - How are you?

NEGATION:

Add "doo" before verb and "da" after verb:
- Doo yá'át'ééh da - It is not good
- Doo naashá da - I am not going
- Doo bizaad yíłti' da - He doesn't speak the language

Examples:
- Doo shił hózhǫ́ da - I am not well / I am unhappy
- Doo yá'át'ééh da - It is not good / bad
- Doo bééhózin da - I don't know

PART 8: DIALECTS AND VARIATION
================================================================================

Navajo has regional dialects, though all are mutually intelligible.

MAJOR DIALECTS:

Eastern dialect (New Mexico):
- Slightly different vocabulary
- Some pronunciation differences
- Example: "sheep" - dibé (east) vs. dibé (west - same in writing, different tone)

Western dialect (Arizona):
- Considered the "standard" for many programs
- Used in most teaching materials
- Example: "water" - tó (same across dialects)

Northern dialect (Utah/Colorado):
- Smallest number of speakers
- Some unique vocabulary
- Influenced by Southern Paiute

CENTRAL NAVAJO (Navajo Nation capital area):
- Blends features of Eastern and Western
- Used in government and media
- What most learners are taught

DIALECT DIFFERENCES:

- Vocabulary: some words vary by region
- Pronunciation: slight tone differences
- Grammar: mostly identical
- Loanwords: varies by neighboring tribes

Learners should focus on one dialect initially, then learn variations.


PART 9: ENDANGERMENT AND REVITALIZATION
================================================================================

CURRENT STATUS:

- UNESCO classification: Definitely Endangered
- Number of speakers: ~148,530 (down from ~200,000 in 1970s)
- Percentage of Navajo population who speak the language: ~54%
- Most fluent speakers are over age 50
- Few children are learning Navajo as their first language
- Navajo is no longer the primary language in most Navajo homes

CAUSES OF LANGUAGE LOSS:

- Boarding schools (forbade Navajo language)
- Economic pressures (English required for jobs)
- Media and technology (primarily English)
- Intermarriage (English-speaking partners)
- Urban migration (away from Navajo communities)
- Shame (past generations were punished for speaking Navajo)

REVITALIZATION EFFORTS:

- Navajo Nation Language Preservation Act (1990)
- Diné Language Immersion Schools
- Navajo Language Teacher Training Program
- Diné College Navajo language degrees
- Navajo Nation Radio (KTNN) - Navajo language programs
- Navajo language apps and online resources
- Head Start Navajo language programs
- Navajo language summer camps
- Master-Apprentice Program (adult learners)
- Navajo Language Academy

WHAT YOU CAN DO:

- Learn Navajo (even basics)
- Support Navajo language programs
- Use Navajo words when possible
- Encourage Navajo speakers to teach
- Advocate for funding
- Share resources
- Respect the language as sacred


PART 10: DINÉ BIZAAD CULTURAL NOTE
================================================================================

The Navajo language is considered sacred.

WHY THE LANGUAGE IS SACRED:

- The Holy People (Diyin Dineʼé) speak Diné Bizaad
- Ceremonies must be performed in Navajo
- The creation story (Diné Bahaneʼ) is in Navajo
- Prayers are ineffective in other languages
- The language itself contains spiritual power

LANGUAGE AND IDENTITY:

- Speaking Navajo is an act of Diné identity
- The language contains Diné worldview
- Concepts like Hózhó cannot be fully translated
- K'é (kinship) is embedded in the grammar
- Clan relationships are expressed through language

RESPECTFUL LANGUAGE LEARNING:

- Acknowledge that Navajo is sacred
- Learn from fluent speakers respectfully
- Do not use ceremonial language casually
- Pay for lessons (language has value)
- Give credit to teachers and sources
- Never mock or parody the language

As the elders say: "Diné Bizaad éí nihił ni' - The Navajo language is our identity."

Sources: Navajo Nation Division of Education, Diné College, Navajo Language Academy, "The Navajo Language" (Young & Morgan), "Diné Bizaad Bínáhoo’aah" (Salina Bookshelf).
