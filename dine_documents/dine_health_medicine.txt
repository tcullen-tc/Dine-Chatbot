================================================================================
DINÉ HEALTH & MEDICINE: TRADITIONAL HEALING, IHS, AND CURRENT HEALTH CHALLENGES
================================================================================
Sources: Navajo Nation Division of Health, Indian Health Service (IHS), "Navajo Healing" (Maureen Trudelle Schwarz)


PART 1: TRADITIONAL DINÉ MEDICINE (NAALYÉHÉ)
================================================================================

Traditional Diné medicine views health holistically (mind, body, spirit, and community).

THE DINÉ VIEW OF HEALTH:

- Health is Hózhó (harmony/balance)
- Illness is imbalance (loss of Hózhó)
- Causes can be physical, spiritual, emotional, or social
- Healing requires restoring Hózhó
- Individual, family, and community all involved

Traditional teaching:
"When you are in harmony with yourself, with others, with nature, and with the Holy People, you are healthy. When you are out of balance, you become ill."

CAUSES OF ILLNESS (Traditional):

Physical causes:
- Injury
- Poisoning
- Contaminated food or water

Spiritual causes:
- Contact with ghosts (ch'į́į́dii)
- Violation of taboos
- Witchcraft
- Offending Holy People
- Contact with forbidden things

Emotional causes:
- Grief
- Anger
- Fear
- Jealousy
- Shock

Social causes:
- Conflict with family
- Broken relationships (K'é)
- Community disharmony


PART 2: THE HATAAŁII (MEDICINE PERSON)
================================================================================

The Hataałii is the traditional healer.

WHAT IS A HATAAŁII?:

- Medicine person / chanter
- Diagnoses illness (spiritual and physical)
- Performs ceremonies
- Uses herbal medicines (naalyéhé)
- Highly respected community leader

HOW ONE BECOMES A HATAAŁII:

1. Called by the Holy People (often through dreams or visions)
2. Apprentices with an experienced Hataałii
3. Training takes 10-20+ years
4. Memorizes hundreds of songs, prayers, sandpaintings, and herbal remedies
5. Must be initiated (final ceremony)
6. Continues learning throughout life

WHAT A HATAAŁII MUST MEMORIZE:

- Hundreds of songs (up to 1,000+)
- Complex sandpainting designs
- Prayer texts
- Herbal medicine knowledge
- Ceremonial protocols
- Diagnostic methods
- Family histories

HATAAŁII SPECIALIZATIONS:

Some Hataałii specialize in specific ceremonies:
- Blessing Way (Hózhóójí)
- Night Way (Tł'éé'jí)
- Enemy Way (Anaa'í)
- Mountain Way (Tó Díínííl)
- Other ceremonies

Some also specialize in:
- Herbal medicine (naalyéhé)
- Hand trembling diagnosis (díínii)
- Star gazing (só' hóyééní)
- Listening diagnosis (hózhǫ́ǫ́gi iiná)


PART 3: DIAGNOSTIC METHODS
================================================================================

Hataałii use various methods to diagnose illness.

HAND TREMBLING (Díínii):

- Medicine person's hand trembles involuntarily
- Trembling points to cause of illness
- Trembling indicates location of illness
- Used for relatively straightforward cases
- Also used to find lost objects or people

STAR GAZING (Só' hóyééní):

- Performed at night (outdoors)
- Medicine person reads signs in the stars
- Stars reveal cause and location of illness
- Used for complex or long-term illnesses
- Also used for important decisions

LISTENING (Hózhǫ́ǫ́gi iiná):

- Medicine person listens to patient's story
- Prayer guides diagnosis
- Holy People reveal cause through listening
- Used for emotional and relationship issues
- Requires deep spiritual connection

PRAYER DIAGNOSIS:

- Medicine person prays for guidance
- Holy People reveal cause in dreams or visions
- Used for most serious cases
- Requires high spiritual attainment
- Not all Hataałii have this gift


PART 4: TRADITIONAL HEALING CEREMONIES
================================================================================

Ceremonies are the primary traditional healing method.

BLESSING WAY (Hózhóójí):

- Most common healing ceremony
- Restores general Hózhó
- Attracts good, health, happiness
- 2-5 days
- Used for prevention as well as healing

NIGHT WAY (Tł'éé'jí):

- Major healing ceremony
- 9 nights
- For serious illness (spiritual causes)
- Treats ghost sickness, lightning, snake illness
- Involves sandpaintings and masked dancers

ENEMY WAY (Anaa'í):

- For illness caused by contact with outsiders
- Also for veterans (returning from war)
- Removes "enemy" contamination
- 2 days (plus public dance)
- Ends with Squaw Dance (public)

MOUNTAIN WAY (Tó Díínííl):

- For bear-related illness
- For injuries from falls
- 5 days
- Involves mountain-themed songs
- Fire dance (final night)

WIND WAY (Nílch'i):

- For respiratory illness
- For wind-related illness
- 2-5 days
- Wind-themed sandpaintings

HORSE WAY (Łį́į́ Naat'ááh):

- For horse-related illness
- For blessing horses
- 2-5 days
- Horse songs


PART 5: HERBAL MEDICINE (NAALYÉHÉ)
================================================================================

Herbal medicines are used alongside ceremonies.

COMMON HERBAL REMEDIES:

YERBA MANSA (Kǫ́ǫ́ nílchi):
- Treats colds and flu
- Anti-inflammatory
- Used in steam baths
- Tea for sore throat
- Poultice for wounds

TOBACCO (Nát'oh):
- Used in offerings and prayers
- Smoked in ceremonies
- Medicinal for respiratory issues
- NEVER used recreationally
- Sacred plant

OAK BARK:
- Tea for stomach issues
- Poultice for wounds
- Astringent properties
- Tannins for tanning

SAGE (Ch'il dóó yis):
- Smudging for purification
- Tea for sore throat
- Used in sweat lodges
- Antibacterial properties
- Sacred plant

JUNIPER (Gad):
- Smoke for purification
- Tea for coughs
- Boughs for ceremonial use
- Berries for medicine
- Sacred tree

RATTLESNAKE ROOT:
- Tea for fever
- Treats snake bites
- Used in Snake Way ceremony
- Powerful medicine (not for casual use)

RULES FOR GATHERING MEDICINES:

- Gather with permission from the plant
- Leave an offering (corn pollen, tobacco)
- Take only what is needed
- Gather respectfully, with prayer
- Thank the plant after gathering
- Do not gather from polluted areas


PART 6: TRADITIONAL PREVENTION
================================================================================

Traditional medicine emphasizes prevention.

DAILY PREVENTION:

- Greet the sun at dawn
- Offer corn pollen
- Pray daily
- Maintain good relationships (K'é)
- Live in Hózhó (harmony/balance)

SEASONAL PREVENTION:

- Blessing Way ceremonies (spring)
- Protection ceremonies (before travel)
- Harvest ceremonies (thanksgiving)
- Winter stories (maintain spiritual balance)

LIFESTYLE PREVENTION:

- Eat traditional foods (when possible)
- Avoid alcohol and drugs
- Respect elders
- Follow traditional teachings
- Participate in ceremonies

PROTECTION FROM GHOSTS (Ch'į́į́dii):

- Avoid burial areas
- Do not speak of the dead (for a period)
- Use corn pollen for protection
- Pray for protection
- Avoid places where someone died


PART 7: THE INDIAN HEALTH SERVICE (IHS)
================================================================================

The Indian Health Service (IHS) is the federal health system for Native Americans.

HISTORY OF IHS:

- Established 1955 (transferred from BIA)
- Responsibility: health care for federally recognized tribes
- Funded by U.S. Congress
- Chronic underfunding (40-60% of need)
- Transfer of authority to tribes (some IHS facilities now tribal)

IHS ON NAVAJO NATION:

- Navajo Area IHS (headquarters in Window Rock, AZ)
- 8 hospitals
- Many clinics and health centers
- Serves Navajo Nation and other tribes in the area

IHS HOSPITALS ON NAVAJO NATION:

- Tuba City Regional Health Care (AZ)
- Chinle Comprehensive Health Care (AZ)
- Fort Defiance Indian Hospital (AZ)
- Gallup Indian Medical Center (NM)
- Northern Navajo Medical Center (Shiprock, NM)
- Crownpoint Healthcare Facility (NM)
- Kayenta Health Center (AZ) (outpatient only)
- Winslow Indian Health Care Center (AZ) (tribal-operated)

IHS CLINICS:

- Many smaller clinics across Navajo Nation
- Provide primary care
- Limited specialty care
- Dental services
- Behavioral health

IHS SERVICES:

- Primary care (checkups, illness, injury)
- Emergency care
- Hospitalization
- Surgery (limited)
- Dental care
- Optometry
- Pharmacy
- Behavioral health (mental health, substance abuse)
- Physical therapy
- Radiology
- Laboratory

IHS FUNDING:

- Per capita funding: approximately $4,000-5,000 per patient (vs. $8,000-10,000 national average)
- Chronic underfunding (40-60% of need)
- Funding determined by Congress (annual appropriations)
- Some facilities tribal-operated (additional funding)
- Private insurance and Medicare/Medicaid also accepted


PART 8: CURRENT HEALTH CHALLENGES
================================================================================

Navajo Nation faces significant health challenges.

DIABETES (Tahoniigee):

- Epidemic proportions
- Approximately 25-30% of adults have diabetes (estimate)
- Higher than national average (10-15%)
- Related to diet, lifestyle, genetics
- Complications: blindness, kidney failure, amputation

Prevention:
- Traditional foods (low sugar, low fat)
- Exercise (walking, herding, traditional activities)
- Weight management
- Diabetes education programs
- Regular screening

Treatment:
- Medication (insulin, oral medications)
- Diet management
- Exercise programs
- Foot care (prevent amputation)
- Kidney dialysis (multiple centers on Navajo Nation)

HEART DISEASE (Bijéí bąą):

- Leading cause of death
- Related to diabetes, diet, lifestyle
- High blood pressure
- High cholesterol
- Limited access to specialty care

CANCER (Ayóó'ánéeshch'id):

- Increasing rates
- Late diagnosis (limited screening)
- Limited oncology services (travel required)
- Uranium mining legacy (lung cancer)
- Environmental exposures

SUBSTANCE ABUSE:

- Alcohol abuse (historical trauma, boarding schools)
- Illicit drugs (methamphetamine, opioids)
- Fentanyl crisis (recent)
- Limited treatment facilities
- Stigma

MENTAL HEALTH:

- High rates of depression and anxiety
- Historical trauma (boarding schools, Long Walk)
- Intergenerational trauma
- Limited mental health providers
- Suicide rates (higher than national average)
- Youth mental health crisis

OBESITY:

- High rates (related to diabetes)
- Diet changes (processed foods, fast food)
- Limited access to healthy foods (food deserts)
- Limited exercise opportunities (some areas)
- Weight management programs

ACCESS TO CARE:

- Long distances to facilities
- Limited transportation
- Limited specialty care (travel to Albuquerque, Phoenix, Salt Lake City)
- Long wait times
- Understaffed facilities
- High turnover of providers

HEALTH DISPARITIES:

- Lower life expectancy (compared to U.S. average)
- Higher infant mortality
- Higher maternal mortality
- Higher rates of preventable diseases
- Limited access to preventive care


PART 9: INTEGRATING TRADITIONAL AND WESTERN MEDICINE
================================================================================

Many Navajos use both traditional and Western medicine.

TRADITIONAL MEDICINE:

- Used alongside Western medicine
- Not exclusive (most use both)
- Covered by IHS? (some ceremonies, limited)
- Private pay (family covers cost)
- Still widely practiced

WESTERN MEDICINE:

- IHS provides standard medical care
- Some specialty care off-reservation
- Emergency care available
- Pharmacy services
- Increasing acceptance of traditional medicine

INTEGRATION:

- Some IHS facilities allow traditional healers
- Cultural liaisons help bridge gap
- Respect for both healing traditions
- Patient choice (use both, either, or neither)
- Growing collaboration

TRADITIONAL MEDICINE AND IHS:

- Some IHS facilities have traditional medicine programs
- Hataałii may be invited to IHS facilities
- Ceremonies not typically covered by IHS
- Families pay for traditional ceremonies
- Some insurance covers (limited)


PART 10: NAVAJO NATION HEALTH INITIATIVES
================================================================================

Navajo Nation is working to improve health.

NAVAJO NATION DIVISION OF HEALTH:

- Oversees health programs
- Coordinates with IHS
- Tribal health policies
- Health promotion
- Disease prevention

HEALTH PROMOTION PROGRAMS:

- Diabetes prevention and management
- Heart disease prevention
- Cancer screening
- Substance abuse treatment
- Mental health services
- Youth wellness programs
- Elder care

COVID-19 RESPONSE (2020-2023):

- Navajo Nation hit hard (high rates)
- Strict lockdowns (curfews, travel restrictions)
- Mask mandates
- Testing and contact tracing
- Vaccination campaigns
- Food and water distribution
- Tribal leadership credited with saving lives

RESULTS:
- Infection rates reduced (compared to surrounding areas)
- Vaccination rates high
- Lessons learned for future pandemics
- Infrastructure improvements (water, food distribution)

SPECIAL PROGRAMS:

- Navajo WIC (women, infants, children)
- Navajo Head Start (health screening)
- Navajo Aging Services (elder care)
- Navajo Cancer Prevention
- Navajo Diabetes Prevention


PART 11: ENVIRONMENTAL HEALTH
================================================================================

Environmental factors affect Navajo health.

URANIUM MINING LEGACY (1940s-1980s):

- Uranium mined on Navajo Nation
- Navajo miners (unprotected)
- Contaminated water and soil
- Abandoned mines (500+)
- Lung cancer, kidney disease, birth defects

CLEANUP:
- Federal legislation (RECA - Radiation Exposure Compensation Act)
- Compensation for affected miners and families
- Ongoing cleanup (EPA, Navajo Nation)
- Still incomplete

WATER CONTAMINATION:

- Abandoned mines (acid drainage)
- Industrial pollution
- Agricultural runoff
- Limited clean water access (some areas)
- Water hauling (common)

AIR QUALITY:

- Coal-fired power plants (Four Corners area)
- Mining operations (dust)
- Vehicle emissions (limited)
- Wood smoke (heating)
- Respiratory illness

CLIMATE CHANGE:

- Drought (water scarcity)
- Extreme heat
- Wildfires (smoke, erosion)
- Changing seasons (affecting ceremonies, traditional foods)
- Adaptation strategies needed


PART 12: HEALTH PHRASES
================================================================================

VOCABULARY:

- Hataałii - Medicine person / chanter
- Naalyéhé - Herbal medicine
- Hózhó - Harmony/balance (health)
- T'óó ahayóí - Illness / disease
- Tahoniigee - Diabetes
- Bijéí bąą - Heart disease
- Ayóó'ánéeshch'id - Cancer
- Tó áłts'íísí - Diarrhea
- Hózhǫ́ǫ́gi át'é - He/she died (literally "became beauty")
- Ch'į́į́dii bits'ą́ą́' - Ghost sickness (illness caused by ghosts)

PHRASES:

- Shił hózhǫ́ - I am well / I am in harmony
- Doo shił hózhǫ́ da - I am unwell / I am out of harmony
- Hataałii shił yá'át'ééh - The medicine person helps me
- Naalyéhé shił yá'át'ééh - The medicine helps me
- T'óó ahayóí shił yá'át'ééh - Illness is hard on me
- T'óó ahayóí doo shił yá'át'ééh da - Illness is not good for me

SEEKING HELP:

- Hataałii baa nitsíjíkees - I am thinking of a medicine person (I want to see one)
- Hataałii yíníyą́ - Go see a medicine person
- T'óó ahayóí baa nitsíjíkees - I am thinking of the illness (I am worried)
- T'óó ahayóí yá'át'ééh - The illness is gone (I am healed)

TRADITIONAL HEALTH SAYINGS:

- "Hózhóogo naashá" - I walk in beauty (health prayer)
- "Hózhó náhásdlį́į́" - Harmony is restored (healing)
- "Shił hózhǫ́" - I am in harmony (I am healthy)

Sources: Navajo Nation Division of Health, Indian Health Service (IHS), "Navajo Healing" (Maureen Trudelle Schwarz), "The Navajo People and Uranium Mining" (Doug Brugge).
