================================================================================
DINÉ CONTEMPORARY ISSUES: CURRENT CHALLENGES, ACTIVISM, AND THE PATH FORWARD
================================================================================
Sources: Navajo Nation Government, Navajo Nation Division of Health, Indigenous Environmental Network, "Diné Perspectives" (University of Arizona Press)


PART 1: INTRODUCTION TO CONTEMPORARY DINÉ ISSUES
================================================================================

The Navajo Nation faces many challenges in the 21st century, but also has many strengths and opportunities.

KEY CHALLENGES:

- Economic development and unemployment
- Infrastructure (water, electricity, internet, roads)
- Health disparities (diabetes, heart disease, substance abuse)
- Language loss (fewer fluent Navajo speakers)
- Education gaps (achievement, graduation rates)
- Environmental issues (uranium cleanup, climate change)
- Housing shortage
- Food insecurity

KEY STRENGTHS:

- Strong cultural identity (K'é, Hózhó)
- Sovereignty (self-governance)
- Natural resources (land, solar, wind)
- Youth activism (growing engagement)
- Language revitalization efforts
- Traditional knowledge (ceremonies, healing)
- Resilience (historical survival)


PART 2: ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT
================================================================================

Economic development remains a major challenge.

UNEMPLOYMENT:

- Estimated 40-50% unemployment on reservation
- Higher in remote areas
- Higher for youth (18-24)
- Higher for those without high school diploma
- Some underemployment (part-time, low-wage, seasonal)

MAJOR EMPLOYERS:

- Navajo Nation government (5,000+ employees)
- Indian Health Service (IHS) (3,000+ employees)
- BIA/Bureau of Indian Education (2,000+ employees)
- Casinos (2,000+ employees)
- Navajo Nation Shopping Centers (500+ employees)
- Small businesses (restaurants, stores, tour companies)

CHALLENGES:

- Limited job opportunities on reservation
- Many commute off-reservation (Gallup, Farmington, Flagstaff)
- Limited access to capital for small businesses
- Bureaucracy (permits, regulations)
- Remote location (far from markets)

OPPORTUNITIES:

- Renewable energy (solar, wind)
- Tourism (growth potential)
- Telecommunications (broadband expansion)
- Small business development
- Arts and crafts (online sales)
- Value-added agriculture


PART 3: INFRASTRUCTURE
================================================================================

Infrastructure on Navajo Nation lags behind the rest of the United States.

WATER:

- 30-40% of homes lack running water (estimate)
- Water hauling common (truck, trailer)
- Contaminated water sources (uranium, arsenic)
- Aging water systems
- Limited funding for new systems

ELECTRICITY:

- 15-20% of homes lack electricity (estimate)
- Solar home systems (some)
- Grid extension slow (remote areas)
- High cost of electricity (some areas)
- Limited funding for grid expansion

INTERNET ACCESS:

- 60-70% lack broadband internet (estimate)
- Limited cell service (remote areas)
- Online learning challenges (students)
- Telehealth challenges (patients)
- Federal funding for expansion (slow)

ROADS:

- Many unpaved roads
- Poor condition (potholes, washboarding)
- Dust (health issue)
- Flood damage (summer storms)
- Limited funding for maintenance

HOUSING:

- Housing shortage (estimated 10,000-20,000 units needed)
- Overcrowding (multiple generations in one home)
- Aging housing stock
- Poor insulation (heat, cold)
- Limited funding for new construction


PART 4: LANGUAGE LOSS AND REVITALIZATION
================================================================================

Diné Bizaad (Navajo language) is endangered.

CURRENT STATUS:

- Approximately 148,530 fluent speakers (as of 2021)
- Most fluent speakers over age 50
- Few children learning Navajo as first language
- Language loss accelerating
- UNESCO classification: Definitely Endangered

CAUSES OF LANGUAGE LOSS:

- Boarding schools (forbade Navajo language)
- English-only education (historical and current)
- Media and technology (primarily English)
- Economic pressures (English required for jobs)
- Intermarriage (English-speaking partners)
- Urban migration (away from Navajo communities)
- Shame (past generations punished for speaking Navajo)

REVITALIZATION EFFORTS:

- Navajo Nation Language Preservation Act (1990)
- Diné Language Immersion Schools (Tséhootsooí, others)
- Navajo Language Teacher Training Program
- Diné College Navajo language degrees
- Navajo Nation Radio (KTNN) - Navajo language programs
- Navajo language apps and online resources
- Head Start Navajo language programs
- Navajo language summer camps
- Master-Apprentice Program (adult learners)
- Navajo Language Academy

SUCCESSES:

- Some immersion school graduates are fluent
- Increased awareness of language loss
- More resources available than 20 years ago
- Youth interest (some)
- Digital resources (apps, websites)

CHALLENGES REMAINING:

- Not enough immersion schools
- Not enough fluent teachers
- Limited funding
- English dominance (media, technology)
- Parental fluency (many parents not fluent)


PART 5: EDUCATION
================================================================================

Education on Navajo Nation faces many challenges.

SCHOOLS:

- 66 BIA/tribal schools (Bureau of Indian Education)
- Many public schools (state-run)
- Some private schools
- Boarding schools (still operating, but fewer)

CHALLENGES:

- Underfunded schools (compared to state average)
- Low graduation rates (compared to national average)
- Achievement gaps (reading, math)
- Limited Navajo language instruction (most schools)
- Teacher shortages
- High teacher turnover
- Remote locations (difficult to recruit teachers)
- Internet access (online learning limited)

SUCCESSES:

- Diné College (first tribal college)
- Navajo Technical University
- Rough Rock Demonstration School (model)
- Tséhootsooí Diné Language Immersion School
- Some high-performing schools

HIGHER EDUCATION:

- Diné College (Tsaile, AZ - main campus)
- Navajo Technical University (Crownpoint, NM)
- Many Navajo students attend off-reservation colleges
- Navajo Nation scholarships available
- Low college completion rates (compared to national average)


PART 6: HEALTH DISPARITIES
================================================================================

Navajo health outcomes lag behind the U.S. average.

DIABETES:

- 25-30% of adults have diabetes (estimate)
- Higher than national average (10-15%)
- Leading cause of kidney failure, amputation
- Prevention programs (limited success)
- Treatment programs (limited resources)

HEART DISEASE:

- Leading cause of death
- Related to diabetes, diet, lifestyle
- Limited access to specialty care
- Prevention programs

SUBSTANCE ABUSE:

- Alcohol abuse (historical trauma)
- Illicit drugs (methamphetamine, opioids)
- Fentanyl crisis (recent)
- Limited treatment facilities
- Stigma

MENTAL HEALTH:

- High rates of depression and anxiety
- Historical trauma (boarding schools, Long Walk)
- Intergenerational trauma
- Limited mental health providers
- Suicide rates (higher than national average)
- Youth mental health crisis

ACCESS TO CARE:

- Long distances to IHS facilities
- Limited specialty care (travel to Albuquerque, Phoenix, Salt Lake City)
- Long wait times
- Understaffed facilities
- High turnover of providers
- Transportation challenges

LIFE EXPECTANCY:

- Lower than U.S. average (approximately 73 years vs. 79 years)
- Infant mortality (higher than U.S. average)
- Maternal mortality (higher than U.S. average)


PART 7: ENVIRONMENTAL ISSUES
================================================================================

Navajo Nation faces significant environmental challenges.

URANIUM MINING LEGACY (1940s-1980s):

- Uranium mined on Navajo Nation
- Navajo miners (unprotected)
- Contaminated water and soil
- Abandoned mines (500+)
- Lung cancer, kidney disease, birth defects

CLEANUP EFFORTS:
- Federal legislation (RECA - Radiation Exposure Compensation Act)
- Compensation for affected miners and families
- Ongoing cleanup (EPA, Navajo Nation)
- Still incomplete (many mines not cleaned)

COAL MINING AND POWER PLANTS:

- Coal mining (Peabody Energy, Black Mesa)
- Coal-fired power plants (Four Corners area)
- Air pollution (respiratory illness)
- Water depletion (slurry pipeline)
- Climate change impacts

CLIMATE CHANGE:

- Drought (water scarcity)
- Extreme heat (heat-related illness)
- Wildfires (smoke, erosion, property loss)
- Changing seasons (affecting ceremonies, traditional foods)
- Adaptation strategies needed

WATER CONTAMINATION:

- Abandoned mines (acid drainage)
- Industrial pollution
- Agricultural runoff
- Limited clean water access (some areas)
- Water hauling (common)

AIR QUALITY:

- Coal-fired power plants (Four Corners area)
- Mining operations (dust)
- Vehicle emissions (limited)
- Wood smoke (heating)
- Respiratory illness


PART 8: ACTIVISM AND MOVEMENTS
================================================================================

Diné activists have been at the forefront of Indigenous rights.

ENVIRONMENTAL JUSTICE:

- Anti-uranium mining activism (1970s-present)
- Anti-coal mining activism (Black Mesa)
- Water rights activism
- Climate justice advocacy

KEY ACTIVISTS:

- Notah Begay III (professional golfer, activist for Indigenous youth health)
- Klee Benally (musician, activist for Indigenous rights, environmental justice)
- Radmilla Cody (musician, activist for domestic violence awareness, Navajo language)
- Winona LaDuke (environmental activist, vice presidential candidate)
- Many local activists (chapter level)

ISSUES ACTIVISTS ADDRESS:

- Environmental contamination
- Language preservation
- Voting rights
- Missing and murdered Indigenous women (MMIW)
- Police brutality
- Boarding school abuses

MMIW (Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women):

- Crisis of missing and murdered Indigenous women
- Navajo women disproportionately affected
- Lack of law enforcement coordination (tribal, federal, state)
- Limited resources for investigation
- Activism to raise awareness
- Federal legislation (Not Invisible Act, Savanna's Act)

COVID-19 ACTIVISM:

- Navajo Nation imposed strict lockdowns (curfews, travel restrictions)
- Mask mandates
- Community mutual aid (food, water distribution)
- Checkpoints (road closures)
- Some resistance (civil liberties)
- Tribal leadership credited with saving lives


PART 9: POLITICAL ISSUES
================================================================================

Navajo Nation faces political challenges.

FEDERAL RELATIONSHIP:

- Government-to-government relationship
- Trust responsibility (federal obligation)
- Chronic underfunding (IHS, BIA, education)
- Congressional appropriations (annual uncertainty)
- Supreme Court decisions (affecting sovereignty)

STATE RELATIONSHIPS:

- Jurisdictional conflicts (tribal vs. state)
- Tax agreements (sales tax, fuel tax)
- Law enforcement cooperation
- Water rights disputes
- Land disputes

TRIBAL GOVERNMENT ISSUES:

- Trust in government (low in some areas)
- Corruption (occasional)
- Turnover (elected officials)
- Bureaucracy
- Need for government reform

VOTING RIGHTS:

- Polling place access (remote areas)
- Language access (Navajo language ballots)
- Voter ID laws (state laws affecting Navajo voters)
- Voter turnout (low)
- Activism to increase participation


PART 10: CULTURAL PRESERVATION
================================================================================

Efforts to preserve Diné culture in the modern world.

LANGUAGE PRESERVATION (covered in Part 4):

- Immersion schools
- Teacher training
- Adult learning programs
- Digital resources

CEREMONIAL PRESERVATION:

- Hataałii training programs
- Youth apprenticeships
- Ceremony funding (some tribal support)
- Recording (with permission, for preservation)
- Intergenerational teaching

TRADITIONAL ARTS PRESERVATION:

- Weaving workshops
- Silversmithing classes
- Pottery classes
- Basketry workshops
- Apprenticeship programs

ARCHIVAL PRESERVATION:

- Navajo Nation Museum (Window Rock)
- Navajo Nation Library
- Navajo Nation Archives
- Oral history projects
- Digital preservation

CHALLENGES:

- Funding (limited)
- Interest (some youth not interested)
- Time (competing with work, school)
- Access (remote locations)
- Appropriation (non-Navajo using Diné culture)


PART 11: YOUTH AND FUTURE
================================================================================

Navajo youth are the future of the Nation.

DEMOGRAPHICS:

- Median age on Navajo Nation: approximately 26-28 years
- Youth population (under 18): approximately 30-35%
- Growing youth population

CHALLENGES FACING YOUTH:

- Educational gaps
- Limited job opportunities
- Substance abuse
- Mental health issues
- Language loss
- Cultural disconnection (some)
- Limited recreational opportunities

STRENGTHS:

- Youth activism (growing)
- Cultural pride (many)
- Bilingual (some)
- Resilience
- Connection to land (many)
- Family support (K'é)

YOUTH PROGRAMS:

- Navajo Nation Youth Council
- Summer youth employment
- Youth leadership programs
- Sports programs
- Cultural camps
- Language immersion camps

VISION FOR THE FUTURE:

- Strong, sovereign Navajo Nation
- Fluent Navajo speakers
- Economic self-sufficiency
- Healthy communities
- Cultural continuity
- Environmental protection
- Quality education for all


PART 12: CONTEMPORARY ISSUES PHRASES
================================================================================

VOCABULARY:

- Naabeehó Bináhásdzo - Navajo Nation
- Naabeehó baa hózhǫ́ - For the good of the Navajo Nation
- Na'nish - Work / job
- Ólta' - School / education
- Tó - Water
- Béésh łigaii - Silver (money)
- K'ad - Now (the present)
- Ałk'idą́ą́' - The past
- T'áá hó ájít'éego - By oneself (self-determination)

PHRASES:

- Naabeehó baa nitsíjíkees - I am thinking of the Navajo Nation
- Naabeehó yá'át'ééh - The Navajo Nation is good
- Naabeehó nizhóní - The Navajo Nation is beautiful
- Naabeehó t'áá hó ájít'éego - The Navajo Nation is self-determining
- NihiK'é baa nitsíjíkees - I am thinking of our family (our people)
- NihiK'é yá'át'ééh - Our family is good

ACTIVISM PHRASES:

- Naabeehó baa shá áná'í - I advocate for the Navajo Nation
- Naabeehó baa na'ní - I work for the Navajo Nation
- Naabeehó baa shił yá'át'ééh - The Navajo Nation helps me
- Naabeehó baa nitsíjíkees - I care about the Navajo Nation

HOPE FOR THE FUTURE:

- Nihił na'anish yá'át'ééh - Our work is good
- Nihił ólta' yá'át'ééh - Our school is good
- Nihił k'é yá'át'ééh - Our family is good
- Nihił hózhó yá'át'ééh - Our harmony is good
