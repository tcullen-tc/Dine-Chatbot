================================================================================
DINÉ GOVERNMENT: NAVAJO NATION STRUCTURE, CHAPTER HOUSES, AND SOVEREIGNTY
================================================================================
Sources: Navajo Nation Government, Navajo Nation Council, Navajo Nation Supreme Court


PART 1: NAVAJO NATION SOVEREIGNTY
================================================================================

The Navajo Nation (Naabeehó Bináhásdzo) is a sovereign nation.

WHAT SOVEREIGNTY MEANS:

- The Navajo Nation has the right to govern itself
- Not subject to state laws (Arizona, New Mexico, Utah)
- Has its own government, courts, and laws
- Has its own police force
- Has its own court system
- Has its own elected officials

TREATY OF 1868:

- Signed June 1, 1868
- Established the Navajo Reservation
- Recognized Navajo Nation as a separate government
- U.S. government promised services (education, healthcare)
- Still in effect today
- The foundation of Navajo sovereignty

Article VI of the Treaty:
"The Navajo Nation shall have the right to govern itself according to its own laws and customs."

RELATIONSHIP WITH THE UNITED STATES:

- Government-to-government relationship
- Navajo Nation deals directly with U.S. government
- Not under state control
- But U.S. government has trust responsibility
- Navajo Nation citizens are U.S. citizens (since 1924)
- Navajo Nation citizens vote in U.S. elections


PART 2: HISTORY OF NAVAJO GOVERNMENT
================================================================================

TRADITIONAL GOVERNMENT (Pre-1868):

- No centralized government
- Local headmen (naat'áanii) led small bands
- Decision-making by consensus
- Clan leaders had authority
- War chiefs for defense
- Peace chiefs for internal matters

THE HEADMAN SYSTEM (Naat'áanii):
- Leader of a local band (extended family group)
- Achieved position through wisdom, generosity, and speaking ability
- Not elected (emerged naturally)
- Led by persuasion, not force
- Made decisions with community input
- Still influential in traditional communities

THE NAVAJO TRIBAL COUNCIL (1923):

- Established by the U.S. government
- Originally to approve oil leases
- First centralized Navajo government
- Members appointed (later elected)
- Evolved into today's government

THE NAVAJO NATION COUNCIL (Modern):
- Established by Tribal Constitution (drafted 1948)
- Ratified by Navajo voters (1950s)
- Reorganized multiple times
- Current structure (2002 - Title 26 reform)

THE NAVAJO NATION CONSTITUTION:

- Written in 1948 (after efforts since 1920s)
- Ratified by Navajo voters in the 1950s
- Established three branches of government
- Has been amended multiple times
- Recognizes traditional Navajo values
- Incorporates K'é and Hózhó into government


PART 3: THREE BRANCHES OF GOVERNMENT
================================================================================

The Navajo Nation has three branches of government (like the U.S.).

1. EXECUTIVE BRANCH (President and Vice President):

President (Presidente):
- Elected by Navajo voters
- 4-year term
- Limited to 2 terms
- Head of government
- Enforces laws
- Represents Navajo Nation to outside governments
- Commander-in-chief of Navajo Nation Police

Vice President (Vice Presidente):
- Elected on same ticket as President
- 4-year term
- Assumes presidency if president unable to serve
- Assists president

DIVISIONS AND DEPARTMENTS:

Executive branch includes:
- Division of Health
- Division of Education
- Division of Social Services
- Division of Natural Resources
- Division of Economic Development
- Division of Public Safety
- Office of the President
- Many other offices

2. LEGISLATIVE BRANCH (Navajo Nation Council):

Navajo Nation Council (Naabeehó Bich'íídáá'):
- 24 delegates (reduced from 88 in 2002)
- Each represents a chapter (or multiple chapters)
- 4-year terms
- Makes laws
- Approves budget
- Confirms presidential appointments

COUNCIL DELEGATES:

- Each delegate represents approximately 6,000 people
- Elections held every 4 years (even years)
- Must be a Navajo Nation citizen
- Must live in the chapter they represent
- Speak Navajo (preferred, not required)

SPEAKER OF THE COUNCIL:

- Elected by Council delegates
- Leads Council meetings
- Sets agenda
- Second in line for presidency (after Vice President)

3. JUDICIAL BRANCH (Courts):

Navajo Nation Supreme Court (Naabeehó Hózhóojí Bee Haz'ą́ą́ Áłahji'):
- Highest court in Navajo Nation
- 3 justices (Chief Justice and 2 Associate Justices)
- Appointed by President (confirmed by Council)
- Life terms (until retirement or removal)
- Reviews lower court decisions

NAVAJO NATION DISTRICT COURTS:

- 7 district courts across Navajo Nation
- Judges appointed by President (confirmed by Council)
- Hear criminal and civil cases
- Jury trials available

NAVAJO NATION PEACE COURTS:

- For minor offenses
- Traditional dispute resolution
- Emphasis on restoration, not punishment
- Community-based

NAVAJO NATION COURT OF APPEALS:

- Intermediate appellate court
- Reviews district court decisions
- Before Supreme Court


PART 4: CHAPTER HOUSES (NA'ALKÁÁDÍ BÁ HOOGHAN)
================================================================================

Chapter houses are the most local level of Navajo government.

WHAT IS A CHAPTER HOUSE?:

- Local government unit (like a county or town)
- Every community has a chapter house
- 110 chapters across Navajo Nation
- Serves a specific geographic area
- Direct contact between people and government

CHAPTER GOVERNMENT:

Each chapter has:
- Chapter President (elected)
- Chapter Vice President (elected)
- Chapter Secretary/Treasurer (elected)
- Chapter Grazing Officer (elected)
- Chapter Land Board (elected)
- Chapter Community Services Coordinator (hired)

CHAPTER MEETINGS:

- Usually monthly (some bi-weekly)
- Open to all chapter members
- Discuss local issues
- Make local decisions
- Vote on resolutions
- Send representatives to Council

CHAPTER POWERS:

- Local land use planning
- Grazing permits
- Community events
- Local ordinances
- Recommend to Council
- Community development

CHAPTER RESPONSIBILITIES:

- Road maintenance
- Water systems
- Community centers
- Senior services
- Youth programs
- Emergency response

FAMOUS CHAPTERS:

- Window Rock (capital)
- Shiprock (largest)
- Tuba City
- Chinle
- Crownpoint
- Kayenta


PART 5: ELECTIONS
================================================================================

Navajo Nation elections are held regularly.

WHO CAN VOTE:

- Navajo Nation citizens
- 18 years or older
- Registered to vote
- Not convicted of a felony (some exceptions)

VOTER REGISTRATION:

- Register at chapter house
- Register at Navajo Election Administration
- Register online (limited)
- Must provide Certificate of Indian Blood (CIB) number

PRESIDENTIAL ELECTIONS:

- Held every 4 years (even years)
- Same year as U.S. presidential elections (but separate)
- Primary election (August)
- General election (November)

Presidential candidates must:
- Be Navajo Nation citizens
- Be at least 35 years old
- Be fluent in Navajo (by law, but enforced variably)
- Not have felony convictions
- Not have been removed from office

COUNCIL ELECTIONS:

- Held every 4 years
- 24 delegates elected
- Each chapter elects its own delegate(s)
- Primary and general elections

CHAPTER ELECTIONS:

- Held every 4 years (sometimes more often)
- Each chapter elects its own officers
- Local issues, local candidates

VOTER TURNOUT:

- Historically low (30-50%)
- Higher for presidential elections
- Lower for chapter elections
- Efforts to increase participation
- Mail-in voting available


PART 6: NAVAJO NATION LAWS (NAABEEHÓ BEE HAZ'Ą́Ą́)
================================================================================

Navajo Nation has its own legal code.

THE NAVAJO NATION CODE:

- Codified laws
- Organized by title (subject)
- Over 20 titles
- Covers criminal, civil, family, property, etc.

TITLE 17 (Navajo Nation Code):
- Elections and voter registration
- Campaign finance
- Ethics rules

TITLE 20 (Navajo Nation Code):
- Courts and judicial procedure
- Evidence rules
- Appeals process

TITLE 26 (Navajo Nation Code):
- Government structure
- Council composition
- Executive branch powers

NAVAJO NATION CRIMINAL CODE:

- Defines crimes (theft, assault, etc.)
- Maximum penalties (usually 1 year in jail)
- Some crimes referred to federal court
- No death penalty

NAVAJO NATION FAMILY LAW:

- Marriage (traditional and civil)
- Divorce
- Child custody
- Child support
- Adoption

NAVAJO NATION ENVIRONMENTAL LAWS:

- Clean air and water
- Mining regulations
- Cultural resource protection
- Sacred sites protection
- Grazing regulations

TRADITIONAL LAW:

- Customary law is recognized
- K'é and Hózhó are legal principles
- Traditional dispute resolution
- Mediation preferred over litigation
- Restoration over punishment


PART 7: NAVAJO NATION POLICE AND PUBLIC SAFETY
================================================================================

Navajo Nation has its own law enforcement.

NAVAJO NATION POLICE (NNPD):

- Established in 1872 (Indian Police)
- Modern force: approximately 200-250 officers
- Jurisdiction: entire Navajo Nation
- Patrols 27,000+ square miles
- Underfunded and understaffed

Police districts:
- Shiprock (Northern)
- Tuba City (Western)
- Chinle (Central)
- Window Rock (Eastern)
- Crownpoint (Southern)

POLICE CHALLENGES:

- Large territory (few officers)
- Rural areas (long response times)
- Limited resources
- High turnover
- Low pay compared to state/local police
- Burnout

OTHER LAW ENFORCEMENT:

- Navajo Nation Criminal Investigations (detectives)
- Navajo Nation SWAT (special weapons)
- Navajo Nation K-9 Unit
- Navajo Nation Search and Rescue
- Navajo Nation Drug Enforcement

FEDERAL LAW ENFORCEMENT ON NAVAJO NATION:

- FBI (major crimes)
- BIA Police (Bureau of Indian Affairs)
- U.S. Marshals
- DEA (drugs)
- ATF (alcohol, tobacco, firearms)

COURT SYSTEM:

- Peace courts (minor offenses)
- District courts (major crimes)
- Supreme Court (appeals)
- No jail for minor offenses (fines only)
- Jail time possible for serious offenses (up to 1 year)

CORRECTIONS:

- Navajo Nation jails in each district
- Overcrowded
- Limited programs
- Some prisoners sent to federal facilities (for longer sentences)
- Reentry programs limited

PART 9: NAVAJO NATION FLAG AND SYMBOLS
================================================================================

The Navajo Nation has official symbols.

NAVAJO NATION FLAG (Naabeehó Bidahwiilʼaah):

Adopted: 1968
Designer: Jay R. Degroat (Navajo)

Design elements:
- Light brown background (sacred earth)
- Four sacred mountains (four directions)
- Rainbow arc (protection)
- Sun (life)
- Corn (sustenance)
- Horses and sheep (wealth)
- Hogan (home)
- Diné people (the people)

The flag represents Diné culture, land, and identity.

NAVAJO NATION SEAL (Naabeehó Binaaltsoos):

Adopted: 1952 (modified 1995)
Design: Raymond Johnson

Design elements:
- Circular shape (the world)
- 50 arrowheads (surrounding)
- Sun (life)
- Corn (sustenance)
- Horses and sheep (wealth)
- Hogan (home)
- Diné people (the people)

The seal is used on official documents, government buildings, and the flag.

NAVAJO NATION MOTTO:

"Naabeehó Bináhásdzo" - The Navajo Nation will survive

(Also: "Hózhóogo Naasháa doo" - I will walk in beauty)


PART 10: RELATIONSHIP WITH STATES AND FEDERAL GOVERNMENT
================================================================================

Navajo Nation interacts with surrounding states.

ARIZONA, NEW MEXICO, UTAH:

- Navajo Nation spans all three states
- Not subject to state laws (on reservation)
- But state laws apply to non-Navajo on reservation (limited)
- Tax agreements with states
- Law enforcement cooperation

Issues with states:
- Water rights
- Land disputes
- Jurisdictional conflicts
- Tax collection
- Education funding

FEDERAL GOVERNMENT:

- Trust responsibility (since treaties)
- Federal agencies provide services (IHS, BIA)
- Federal funding (through appropriations)
- Congressional oversight
- Supreme Court decisions affect Navajo Nation

FEDERAL AGENCIES ON NAVAJO NATION:

- Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA)
- Indian Health Service (IHS)
- Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI - major crimes)
- Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)
- Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD)


PART 11: CURRENT ISSUES AND CHALLENGES
================================================================================

The Navajo Nation government faces many challenges.

FUNDING:

- Chronic underfunding
- Federal appropriations insufficient
- Tax base limited (on reservation)
- Economic development challenges
- Dependence on federal funding

INFRASTRUCTURE:

- Many homes without electricity
- Many homes without running water
- Poor road conditions
- Limited internet access
- Aging facilities

HEALTH:

- High rates of diabetes
- High rates of heart disease
- Limited access to specialty care
- Mental health services limited
- Substance abuse

EDUCATION:

- Underfunded schools
- Low graduation rates (compared to national average)
- Limited higher education access
- Language loss (younger generations not fluent)
- Need for culturally relevant curriculum

ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT:

- High unemployment (40-50%)
- Limited job opportunities
- Reliance on federal funding and casinos
- Need for diversified economy
- Entrepreneurship limited

JUSTICE:

- Underfunded police
- Understaffed courts
- Overcrowded jails
- High crime rates (some areas)
- Need for restorative justice programs

GOVERNANCE:

- Trust in government (low in some areas)
- Turnover (elected officials)
- Corruption (occasional)
- Bureaucracy
- Need for government reform

PART 13: NAVAJO NATION TODAY
================================================================================

The Navajo Nation today (2025):

POPULATION:

- Enrolled members: over 250,000
- Living on reservation: approximately 150,000
- Largest tribe in the United States

LAND:

- 27,000+ square miles
- Largest reservation in the United States
- Spans Arizona, New Mexico, Utah
- Also trust land in other states

ECONOMY:

- Budget: approximately $500 million - $1 billion (varies)
- Major employers: Navajo Nation government, IHS, BIA, casinos
- Natural resources: coal, oil, gas, uranium (declining)
- Tourism: Monument Valley, Canyon de Chelly, Four Corners

GOVERNMENT:

- 24 Council delegates
- 110 chapters
- 7 district courts
- 1 Supreme Court

EDUCATION:

- 66 BIA/tribal schools
- Many public schools (state-run)
- Diné College (tribal college)
- Navajo Technical University
- Many students attend off-reservation colleges

FUTURE CHALLENGES:

- Economic diversification
- Infrastructure development
- Language preservation
- Youth engagement
- Government reform

Sources: Navajo Nation Government, Navajo Nation Council, Navajo Nation Supreme Court, "Navajo Nation Government" (David E. Wilkins), "The Navajo Political Experience" (David E. Wilkins).
