================================================================================
DINÉ SACRED PLACES: THE FOUR SACRED MOUNTAINS, CANYON DE CHELLY, AND HOLY SITES
================================================================================
Sources: Navajo Nation Historic Preservation Department, Navajo Nation Cultural Resources, "The Sacred Mountains of the Navajo" (John R. Farella)


PART 1: THE FOUR SACRED MOUNTAINS
================================================================================

The four sacred mountains were placed by the Holy People (Diyin Dineʼé) to mark the boundaries of Dinétah (Navajo homeland).

According to Diné creation stories:
- The mountains were created from earth brought from the lower worlds
- Each mountain was given a sacred stone, color, and direction
- The mountains protect the Diné people
- They are living beings, not just landmarks

When the Diné were created, the Holy People said:
"These mountains are your boundaries. Do not go beyond them. Within them, you will be protected. Beyond them, you will be vulnerable."


MOUNTAIN 1: EAST - SISNAAJINÍ (BLANCA PEAK)
================================================================================

Location: Colorado (near Alamosa)
Elevation: 14,351 feet (4,374 meters)
Sacred stone: White shell (Yoołgai)
Sacred color: White
Direction: East
Season: Spring
Time of day: Dawn

Spiritual meaning:
- Represents new beginnings
- Associated with thinking (Nitsáhákees)
- Where the first man and woman were created
- Home of the White Shell Holy People

Traditional story:
In the creation story, First Man and First Woman were created on Sisnaajiní. The mountain was placed first, establishing the eastern boundary.

What the mountain represents:
- Dawn and the beginning of life
- White shell (symbol of purity and abundance)
- The mind and thinking
- The direction of emergence

Sacred sites on the mountain:
- The summit (not to be visited casually)
- Sacred springs
- Prayer offering locations

Today: The mountain is on private land (not open to the public for ceremonial access without permission).


MOUNTAIN 2: SOUTH - TSOODZIŁ (MOUNT TAYLOR)
================================================================================

Location: New Mexico (near Grants)
Elevation: 11,301 feet (3,445 meters)
Sacred stone: Turquoise (Dootł'izh)
Sacred color: Blue
Direction: South
Season: Summer
Time of day: Day

Spiritual meaning:
- Represents water and life
- Associated with planning (Nahat'á)
- Home of the Turquoise Holy People
- Source of sacred springs

Traditional story:
The Holy People placed Tsoodził to provide water to the Diné. The mountain's springs are considered sacred.

What the mountain represents:
- Day and the fullness of life
- Turquoise (sky, water, protection)
- Planning and purpose
- Abundance of water

Sacred sites on the mountain:
- Sacred springs
- Caves used for ceremonies
- Prayer offering locations

Today: Most of Mount Taylor is on public land (Cibola National Forest). Ceremonial access is permitted with respect.


MOUNTAIN 3: WEST - DOOKʼOʼOOSŁÍÍD (SAN FRANCISCO PEAKS)
================================================================================

Location: Arizona (near Flagstaff)
Elevation: 12,633 feet (3,851 meters)
Sacred stone: Abalone shell (Díchíí)
Sacred color: Yellow
Direction: West
Season: Fall
Time of day: Evening

Spiritual meaning:
- Represents the end of the day and harvest
- Associated with living (Iiná)
- Home of the Abalone Shell Holy People
- Most sacred of the four mountains

Traditional story:
The Holy People placed Dookʼoʼoosłííd as the western boundary. The mountain is considered the home of many Holy People.

What the mountain represents:
- Evening and the end of the day
- Abalone shell (beauty and wealth)
- Life and living fully
- The harvest of life

Sacred sites on the mountain:
- The summit (most sacred)
- The inner basin (ceremonial area)
- Sacred springs
- Prayer offering locations

Important note: The San Francisco Peaks are the most sacred of the four mountains. The summit is not to be climbed casually. The peaks are used for ceremonies including the Night Way (Tł'éé'jí).


MOUNTAIN 4: NORTH - DIBÉ NITSAA (HESPERUS PEAK)
================================================================================

Location: Colorado (near Durango)
Elevation: 13,232 feet (4,033 meters)
Sacred stone: Jet (Bááshzhinii)
Sacred color: Black
Direction: North
Season: Winter
Time of day: Night

Spiritual meaning:
- Represents night and protection
- Associated with assurance (Sihasin)
- Home of the Jet Holy People
- Protects the Diné from the north

Traditional story:
The Holy People placed Dibé Nitsaa as the northern boundary. The mountain protects the Diné from cold winds and enemies.

What the mountain represents:
- Night and the end of the day
- Jet (protection and strength)
- Assurance and security
- The final direction

Sacred sites on the mountain:
- The summit
- Sacred springs
- Prayer offering locations

Today: The mountain is on private land (not open to the public for ceremonial access without permission).


PART 2: THE CENTER - DINÉTAH AND THE MIDDLE MOUNTAINS
================================================================================

The Diné homeland is the area within the four sacred mountains.

DINÉTAH (traditional Navajo homeland):
- The area bounded by the four sacred mountains
- Located primarily in northwestern New Mexico
- Includes Canyon de Chelly, Chaco Canyon, and other sacred sites
- The place where the Diné emerged into this world

According to tradition:
"This is the center of the world. The four mountains protect us. Within them, we are safe. Outside them, we are vulnerable."

THE MIDDLE MOUNTAINS (between the four):

Two additional mountains were created in the middle of the four directions:

CHÓĬLI (also known as Gobernador Knob):
- Location: New Mexico (near the Colorado border)
- Made from the remaining earth after creating the four mountains
- Colored with a mixture of white shell, turquoise, abalone, and jet
- Represents the center of the Diné world

TZĬLHNÚHODĬHLĬ (also known as Huerfano Mountain):
- Location: New Mexico (near Blanco)
- Also made from remaining earth
- Colored with a mixture of all sacred stones
- Represents the heart of Dinétah


PART 3: CANYON DE CHELLY (TSÉYIʼ)
================================================================================

Canyon de Chelly (Tséyiʼ - "The Rock Canyon") is one of the most sacred places in Diné culture.

Location: Northeastern Arizona (within Navajo Nation)
Length: 26 miles (42 kilometers)
Depth: Up to 1,000 feet (305 meters)
Sacred significance: The place where the Diné first lived after emergence

SPIRITUAL MEANING:

Canyon de Chelly is considered the birthplace of the Diné people.

According to tradition:
- The Diné emerged into this world at Canyon de Chelly
- The Holy People taught the Diné how to live in this canyon
- Spider Woman taught weaving at Canyon de Chelly
- The Hero Twins defeated monsters in the canyon
- The canyon walls contain prayers in the form of petroglyphs

SACRED SITES WITHIN CANYON DE CHELLY:

Spider Rock (Na'ashjé'ii Asdzáá Tsé):
- An 800-foot sandstone spire
- Home of Spider Woman (Na'ashjé'ii Asdzáá)
- She taught weaving from this location
- The white cap represents the sun's light
- The dark base represents the earth

Antelope House (Kin Dah Łichí'í):
- Ancient pueblo ruins
- Petroglyphs of antelope
- Sacred ceremonial site
- Evidence of ancient Diné occupation

Mummy Cave (Tsé Yázhí):
- Well-preserved ancient dwellings
- Human remains found (sacred)
- Not open to the public
- Ceremonial site

White House Ruins (Kin Łigaaí):
- Ancient pueblo ruins
- Accessible via trail
- Sacred to both Diné and Pueblo peoples
- Used for ceremonies

Standing Cow Ruins:
- Petroglyph of a cow
- Sacred ceremonial area
- Evidence of Spanish contact

Sliding House Ruins:
- Ruins built into cliff face
- Sacred site
- Not open to the public

VISITING CANYON DE CHELLY:

Canyon de Chelly is a National Monument, but it is also a living Navajo community.

Rules for visitors:
- Stay on designated trails
- Do not climb on ruins
- Do not touch petroglyphs
- Do not remove anything (rocks, artifacts, plants)
- Respect Navajo residents (do not enter homes)
- Hire Navajo guides for backcountry access
- Photography is allowed but be respectful
- Do not visit ceremonial sites without permission

Sacred protocol:
- The canyon is not just a park - it is a home
- Navajo families live in the canyon
- Ceremonies are still performed there
- Ask permission before photographing people
- Offer corn pollen or tobacco when praying


PART 4: CHACO CANYON (TSÉ BIT'A'Í)
================================================================================

Chaco Canyon (Tsé Bit'a'í - "Rock with Wings") is a sacred place in Diné history.

Location: Northwestern New Mexico
Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site

DINÉ CONNECTION:

- Chaco Canyon was built by Ancestral Pueblo people (ancestors of modern Pueblo peoples)
- The Diné consider Chaco a sacred place
- Chaco is mentioned in Diné origin stories
- The Diné did not build Chaco, but it is part of Diné history
- Some Diné clans trace ancestry to Chaco

SPIRITUAL MEANING:

According to Diné tradition:
- The Hero Twins visited Chaco Canyon
- The Holy People taught ceremonies there
- The canyon is a place of power

SACRED SITES:

Pueblo Bonito:
- Largest great house at Chaco
- Sacred ceremonial center
- Aligned with solstices and equinoxes

Fajada Butte:
- Contains the Sun Dagger petroglyph
- Marks the solstices
- Sacred calendar site

Casa Rinconada:
- Great kiva (ceremonial structure)
- Aligned with celestial events
- Still used for ceremonies

VISITING CHACO CANYON:

- Chaco Canyon is a National Historical Park
- It is sacred to multiple tribes (Diné, Pueblo, Hopi)
- Visitors must stay on trails
- Do not enter kivas without permission
- Do not touch petroglyphs
- Photography is allowed but be respectful


PART 5: SHIPROCK (TSÉ BIT'A'Í)
================================================================================

Shiprock (Tsé Bit'a'í - "Rock with Wings") is a sacred rock formation in New Mexico.

Location: Northwestern New Mexico (near Shiprock, NM)
Elevation: 7,177 feet (2,188 meters)
Height above plain: 1,583 feet (483 meters)

DINÉ NAME: Tsé Bit'a'í ("Rock with Wings")

The name comes from the legend that the rock was once a bird that carried the Diné to safety.

THE LEGEND OF SHIPROCK:

According to Diné tradition:
- The Diné were being attacked by enemies
- A giant bird carried the people to safety on its back
- The bird turned to stone, becoming Shiprock
- The "wings" are the rock formations extending from the main spire
- The Diné settled in the area after being carried to safety

SPIRITUAL MEANING:

- Shiprock is a sacred place of refuge
- It represents protection and salvation
- The rock is a living being (not just a geological formation)
- Ceremonies are still performed near Shiprock
- The summit is sacred and not to be climbed

RESTRICTIONS:

- Climbing Shiprock is prohibited by Navajo Nation law
- The rock is sacred, not a recreation site
- Violators may be prosecuted
- Respect the Navajo Nation's closure of the site


PART 6: DINOSAUR TRACKS (NAASHJÉII ASDZÁÁ BIKÉÉ' - SPIDER WOMAN'S TRACKS)
================================================================================

Dinosaur tracks are found throughout Dinétah and are considered sacred.

LOCATIONS:

- Tuba City, Arizona (visible tracks)
- Chaco Canyon area
- Various locations on Navajo Nation

DINÉ TRADITIONAL INTERPRETATION:

According to Diné oral tradition:
- The tracks are not dinosaurs (a Western concept)
- They are the tracks of Holy People (Diyin Dineʼé)
- Some are called "Naashjéii Asdzáá Bikéé'" (Spider Woman's tracks)
- Others are tracks of Monster Slayer (Naayééʼ Neizghání)
- The tracks are prayers in stone

SPIRITUAL MEANING:

- The tracks are sacred, not scientific curiosities
- They represent the presence of Holy People
- Some tracks are used in ceremonies
- The tracks teach about the power of the Holy People

VISITING DINOSAUR TRACKS:

- Many tracks are on Navajo Nation land
- Some are accessible to the public (Tuba City)
- Others are on private or restricted land
- Do not remove or damage tracks
- Do not make casts or rubbings without permission
- The tracks are not for commercial exploitation


PART 7: PETROGLYPHS AND PICTOGRAPHS (TSÉ YIDÉÍNÍGÍÍ)
================================================================================

Petroglyphs (carved rock art) and pictographs (painted rock art) are found throughout Dinétah.

SACRED MEANING:

According to Diné tradition:
- The images were not made by humans alone
- Holy People created some of them
- They are prayers and teachings in stone
- They mark sacred places
- They contain spiritual power

TYPES OF IMAGES:

- Holy People (Diyin Dineʼé)
- Hero Twins (Monster Slayer and Born for Water)
- Coyote (Ma'ii)
- Animals (bear, deer, antelope, sheep)
- Spirals (represent emergence)
- Footprints and handprints
- Geometric designs (represent the four directions)
- Ceremonial figures

FAMOUS PETROGLYPH SITES:

- Canyon de Chelly (many locations)
- Chaco Canyon (Fajada Butte, others)
- Dinosaur tracks sites
- Various locations throughout Dinétah

PROTOCOL FOR VISITING:

- Do not touch petroglyphs (oils damage rock)
- Do not trace or chalk petroglyphs
- Do not remove rocks with petroglyphs
- Do not photograph for commercial use without permission
- Respect the sacred nature of the images
- Some sites are closed to the public


PART 8: OTHER SACRED PLACES
================================================================================

WINDOW ROCK (TSÉGHÁHOODZÁNÍ):

Location: Arizona (Navajo Nation capital)
Sacred meaning: "The rock with a hole through it"
- A natural arch formation
- Represents the gateway to Dinétah
- The Navajo Nation government is located here
- Sacred ceremonies are performed near Window Rock

MONUMENT VALLEY (TSÉ BIIʼ NIDZISGAI):

Location: Arizona/Utah border
Sacred meaning: "The valley of the rocks"
- Home of the Hero Twins
- Where Monster Slayer (Naayééʼ Neizghání) defeated the monsters
- The buttes represent the Hero Twins and their weapons
- Sacred site for ceremonies

RAINBOW BRIDGE (NONOZHO):

Location: Utah (within Navajo Nation)
Sacred meaning: "Rainbow turned to stone"
- Natural arch
- Sacred to Diné and other tribes
- Considered a living being
- Not to be walked under without permission

CHACO CANYON (TSÉ BIT'A'Í):

Location: New Mexico
Sacred meaning: "Rock with wings"
- Sacred ceremonial center
- Mentioned in Diné origin stories
- Important for multiple tribes

COMB RIDGE (TSÉ ŁICHÍÍ'Í):

Location: Utah
Sacred meaning: "Red rock"
- Sacred ceremonial site
- Contains petroglyphs
- Used for ceremonies

NAVAJO NATIONAL MONUMENT (TSÉ BIT'A'Í):

Location: Arizona
Sacred meaning: "Rock with wings" (similar to Shiprock)
- Ancient cliff dwellings
- Sacred to both Diné and Pueblo peoples
- Interpretive trails


PART 9: PROPER BEHAVIOR AT SACRED PLACES
================================================================================

When visiting sacred Diné places, follow these protocols:

DO:
- Be respectful and quiet
- Stay on designated trails
- Ask permission (if appropriate)
- Leave offerings of corn pollen or tobacco (if you know how)
- Take only photographs (with permission)
- Leave only footprints
- Learn about the site before visiting
- Hire Navajo guides when available

DO NOT:
- Climb on sacred rocks or ruins
- Touch petroglyphs or pictographs
- Remove anything (rocks, artifacts, plants)
- Make loud noises or play music
- Bring alcohol or drugs
- Photograph ceremonies (without permission)
- Visit sites that are closed
- Ignore posted restrictions

SPECIFIC RESTRICTIONS:

- Shiprock: Climbing is prohibited
- Canyon de Chelly: Some areas require a guide
- Chaco Canyon: Stay on trails
- Dinosaur tracks: Do not touch or cast
- Petroglyphs: Do not touch or trace
- Four Sacred Mountains: Summits are not to be climbed casually


PART 10: SACRED PLACES AND CLIMATE CHANGE
================================================================================

Climate change is affecting sacred Diné places.

IMPACTS:

- Drought drying up sacred springs
- Wildfires burning sacred areas
- Erosion damaging petroglyphs
- Invasive plants at sacred sites
- Changing seasons affecting ceremonies

PRESERVATION EFFORTS:

- Navajo Nation Historic Preservation Department
- Sacred Sites Protection Program
- Tribal consultation for federal projects
- Monitoring and documentation
- Climate adaptation planning

HOW YOU CAN HELP:

- Support Navajo Nation preservation efforts
- Respect closures and restrictions
- Visit responsibly (low impact)
- Donate to preservation organizations
- Advocate for protection of sacred sites
- Learn about the sites before visiting
