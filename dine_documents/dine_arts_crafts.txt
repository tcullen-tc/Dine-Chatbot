DINÉ ARTS & CRAFTS: WEAVING, SILVERSMITHING, POTTERY, AND BASKETRY
================================================================================
Sources: Navajo Nation Cultural Resources, Museum of Northern Arizona, Wheelwright Museum


PART 1: NAVAJO WEAVING (DIYIN DINEʼÉ BINAALTSOOS)
================================================================================

Navajo weaving is a sacred tradition taught to the Diné by Spider Woman (Na'ashjé'ii Asdzáá).

THE LEGEND OF SPIDER WOMAN:

According to Diné oral tradition:
- Spider Woman taught the Navajo people how to weave
- The first loom was made of sky and earth
- The weaving tools were made of sunlight, lightning, and rain
- Spider Woman said: "The weaving is a prayer. Every thread is a thought. Every pattern is a story."
- She taught that weavers should leave a "spirit line" (Chʼihóníʼįį) to let their spirit escape from the weaving

THE SPIRIT LINE (Chʼihóníʼįį):
- A small thread from the center of the weaving to the edge
- Purpose: Allows the weaver's spirit to escape from the weaving
- Without it, the weaver might be trapped in their own creation
- Traditional weavings always include a spirit line
- Modern weavings sometimes omit it, but traditional weavers maintain the practice

THE WEAVING PROCESS:

1. SHEARING (Dibé sháń beʼestłʼin):
   - Sheep are sheared in spring
   - Wool is sorted by quality
   - Best wool comes from the neck and shoulders

2. CLEANING (Tó bee yíłtłééh):
   - Wool is washed in water
   - Dirt and grease are removed
   - Wool is dried in the sun

3. CARDING (Bee atłʼóhí):
   - Wool fibers are straightened
   - Short fibers are removed
   - Wool is rolled into roving

4. SPINNING (Yistłééh):
   - Wool is twisted into yarn using a spindle
   - Traditional spindles are made of wood
   - The spinner walks backward as they spin, pulling the yarn

5. DYEING (Tó bee yíníłchí):
   - Natural dyes from plants, minerals, and insects
   - Indigo for blue (traded from Mexico)
   - Cochineal for red (from cactus insects)
   - Wolf moss for yellow
   - Sumac for black
   - Modern weavers also use commercial dyes

6. WARPING (Yikáí):
   - Vertical threads are set up on the loom
   - Warp determines the length of the weaving
   - Traditional looms are upright (not horizontal like European looms)

7. WEAVING (Atłʼó):
   - Weft threads are woven horizontally
   - The weaver sits on the ground, leaning forward
   - A batten (flat wooden tool) separates the warp threads
   - A comb beats the weft threads into place

8. FINISHING:
   - The weaving is removed from the loom
   - Ends are knotted or hemmed
   - The spirit line is the last thing woven

THE LOOM (Náłtsoos bikáá' dah asdáhí):
- Upright loom (not horizontal like European looms)
- Made of wooden poles
- The weaver sits on the ground
- The warp is wound around the top and bottom poles
- Traditional looms are portable

Parts of the loom:
- Top and bottom poles (holds the warp)
- Side poles (keeps tension)
- Batten (wooden sword to separate warp)
- Comb (to beat weft threads)
- Shuttle (holds weft yarn)


PART 2: WEAVING PATTERNS AND THEIR MEANINGS
================================================================================

Each weaving pattern has traditional meanings:

STORM PATTERN (Yoołgai Asdzą́ą́ Nááhai):
- Represents the four sacred mountains and directions
- Zigzag lines represent lightning
- Diamond shapes represent the four directions
- Cross shapes represent the four seasons
- The center represents the middle of the Diné world

EYE DAZZLER (Náhookǫs Baʼáád):
- Bright, geometric patterns with contrasting colors
- Designed to "dazzle" the eyes
- Uses repeating diamond or zigzag patterns
- Often uses primary colors (red, yellow, blue)
- Popular in late 19th century

CHIEF'S BLANKET (Hózhǫ́ǫ́gi atłʼó):
- Traditional striped patterns
- Worn by leaders and warriors
- Three distinct phases (I, II, III)
- Phase I: simple brown and blue stripes
- Phase II: added red stripes
- Phase III: added diamonds and zigzags

TWO GREY HILLS (Naʼnízhoozhíí Łichíí'í):
- Natural, undyed wool in shades of brown, white, and gray
- Named after the trading post near Two Grey Hills, NM
- No commercial dyes - only natural colors
- Very fine, tight weave
- Considered the most prestigious style

GANADO (Naʼnízhoozhíí Łigaii):
- Red background with geometric designs
- Named after the trading post in Ganado, AZ
- Popularized by trader J.L. Hubbell
- Deep red background (from commercial dye)
- Black, white, and gray geometric patterns

CRYSTAL (Tsééhííł):
- Fine weave with vegetal dyes
- Named after the trading post in Crystal, NM
- Soft, muted colors from natural dyes
- Complex, repeating patterns
- Developed in early 20th century

WIDE RUINS (Kin Łigaii):
- Bold geometric patterns
- Named after the trading post in Wide Ruins, AZ
- Soft, pastel colors
- Horizontal bands with geometric designs
- Developed in 1940s

TEEC NOS POS (Tééʼ Náázhnízóó):
- A combination of Two Grey Hills and Crystal styles
- Named after the trading post in Teec Nos Pos, AZ
- Very complex, detailed patterns
- Many small repeating elements
- Considered the most intricate style

Colors and their meanings:
- White (East): dawn, new beginnings, white shell
- Blue (South): day, water, turquoise
- Yellow (West): evening, harvest, abalone
- Black (North): night, protection, jet
- Red: life, blood, fire
- Green: plants, growth, spring


PART 3: FAMOUS WEAVING TRADITIONS
================================================================================

WEAVING CLANS:
- The Weaver Clan (Atłʼóó Dineʼé) is one of the Navajo clans
- Weaving knowledge is passed through families
- Some families are known for specific styles

HISTORICAL WEAVING PERIODS:

Classic Period (1700-1860):
- Chief's blankets
- Simple striped patterns
- Natural brown, white, blue, and indigo
- Woven for personal use, not trade

Transitional Period (1860-1890):
- Introduction of red (Cochineal dye)
- More complex patterns
- Germantown yarns (commercial, bright colors)
- Eye dazzler patterns

Regional Period (1890-1930):
- Distinct regional styles develop
- Trading posts influence designs
- Two Grey Hills, Ganado, Crystal styles emerge
- Rugs become primary product

Revival Period (1930-present):
- Return to natural dyes
- Fine weaving encouraged
- Traditional patterns revived
- New styles continue to develop

Notable weavers:
- Hosteen Klah (1867-1937) - famous for Yé'iibicheii (Talking God) designs
- Daisy Taugelchee (1909-1990) - Two Grey Hills master
- Mary Lee Begay - Ganado style expert
- Barbara Teller Ornelas - traditional weaver, author
- Roy T. Begay - contemporary weaver


PART 4: NAVAJO SILVERSMITHING (Béésh Łigaii Yił Naalʼaad)
================================================================================

Navajo silversmithing began in the 1850s after learning from Mexican smiths.

HISTORY:

Before silver:
- Diné wore shell and turquoise jewelry
- Beads made from shell, stone, and bone
- Trading with Pueblo people for turquoise

Learning silversmithing (1850s):
- Atsidi Sani (Old Smith) learned from a Mexican smith
- He taught other Navajo smiths
- By 1880, silversmithing was widespread
- Initially used Mexican silver coins (melted down)

The Concho Belt (1860s):
- One of the first Navajo silver items
- Made of silver discs (conchos) linked together
- Worn by both men and women
- Often decorated with stampwork

The Squash Blossom Necklace (1870s):
- Inspired by Spanish and Mexican designs
- Silver beads alternating with "squash blossom" pendants
- The naja (crescent pendant) at the bottom
- Traditional wedding necklace

SILVERSMITHING TECHNIQUES:

CASTING (Tó ééłtʼééh):
- Silver is melted in a crucible
- Poured into a mold (sand or tufa stone)
- Tufa casting: carved into volcanic stone
- Each casting is unique
- Used for bracelets, pendants, buttons

REPOUSSÉ (Béésh nidaalchíí):
- Silver is hammered from the back
- Creates raised designs
- Used for conchos, bracelets, and buckles
- Requires great skill

STAMPWORK (Béésh bee nidaʼoolééh):
- Stamps are struck with a hammer
- Creates repeating patterns
- Many traditional Navajo stamps exist
- Used for bracelets, conchos, and beads

FILIGREE (Béésh nidaatłʼo):
- Fine silver wire is shaped into designs
- Soldered onto a silver base
- Delicate, lace-like appearance
- Became popular in 20th century

INLAY (Béésh biih yidlééh):
- Turquoise, coral, or shell set into silver
- Silver is cut to fit the stone
- Stone is held by bezels (silver walls)
- The most common technique today

STONES AND MATERIALS:

Turquoise (Dootłʼizh):
- Most sacred stone for Diné
- Represents the sky and water
- Colors: blue, green, white (rare)
- Source: New Mexico, Arizona, Nevada
- Believed to protect the wearer

Coral (Dzilnéz):
- Represent red (life, blood, fire)
- Traded from the Mediterranean
- Used since ancient times
- Often paired with turquoise

Shell (Yoołgai):
- White shell represents the East
- Abalone shell represents the West
- Used for beads and pendants
- Traded from the Pacific coast

Jet (Bááshzhinii):
- Black stone represents the North
- Found in New Mexico and Arizona
- Used for contrast with turquoise
- Polished to a high shine


PART 5: FAMOUS SILVERSMITHS
================================================================================

ATSIDI SANI (Old Smith, 1828-1918):
- First Navajo silversmith
- Learned from Mexican smiths
- Taught his sons and other Navajo
- Made simple silver beads and conchos

SLIM STAMP (1894-1976):
- Famous for stampwork designs
- Developed many new stamp patterns
- His work is highly collectible
- Worked well into his 80s

KENNETH BEGAY (1919-1978):
- Master of tufa casting
- Created innovative bracelet designs
- Won many awards at Gallup Ceremonial
- His work is in museums

TOM BURNSIDE (born 1950):
- Contemporary silversmith
- Known for traditional stampwork
- Uses old-style tools and techniques
- His work honors traditional methods

VERNON BEGAY (born 1960):
- Master of the naja (crescent pendant)
- Creates traditional wedding necklaces
- Uses tufa casting and stampwork
- His work is in major collections


PART 6: NAVAJO POTTERY (Ásaaʼ)
================================================================================

Navajo pottery is less known than weaving or silversmithing but has ancient roots.

TRADITIONAL POTTERY:
- Made by women (traditionally)
- Coil method (not wheel)
- Fired in open pits
- No glaze - natural finish
- Used for cooking, storage, water

SHAPES:
- Water jars (high-necked)
- Cooking pots (wide-mouthed)
- Storage jars (large)
- Bowls and cups

DECORATION:
- Simple incised lines
- Scalloped rims
- Corrugated textures
- Sometimes painted with clay slips

COLORS:
- Natural clay color (tan, brown, red)
- Black (from smothered firing)
- Red clay slip (iron oxide)

REVIVAL OF POTTERY:
- 1970s: Navajo pottery revival began
- Artists learned from elders
- New styles developed
- Today, pottery is collectible art

Notable potters:
- Alice Cling (contemporary)
- Rose Williams (revival leader)
- Christine McHorse (innovator)
- Barbara Gonzales (award-winning)


PART 7: NAVAJO BASKETRY (Ndaa')
================================================================================

Navajo basketry is ancient and primarily ceremonial.

TYPES OF BASKETS:

Ceremonial Baskets (Tłʼééʼjí naadaaʼ):
- Used in Night Way ceremonies
- Made from sumac (Rhus trilobata)
- Coiled construction
- Designs represent sacred mountains and directions

Wedding Baskets (Naadą́ą́' ííkʼǫ́):
- Used in traditional weddings
- Same construction as ceremonial baskets
- Symbolizes the union of families
- Often given as gifts

Storage Baskets:
- Made from willow or rabbitbrush
- Twined construction
- Used for storing food or goods
- Less common today

BASKET DESIGNS:
- Center dot: the place of emergence
- Spiral lines: the path of life
- Stepped triangles: the four sacred mountains
- Colors: natural (tan, brown, black)

How baskets are made:
- Sumac is gathered in fall
- Stems are split and soaked
- Coiled around a bundle of grass
- Stitches hold the coil together
- Designs are created with dyed stems

Notable basket makers:
- Ella Mae Begay
- Dorothy Goldtooth
- Mabel Burnside Myers
- Barbara Teller Ornelas


PART 8: NAVAJO ARTS TODAY
================================================================================

Diné arts continue to evolve while honoring tradition.

CONTEMPORARY ARTISTS:

R.C. GORMAN (1931-2005):
- Famous Navajo painter
- Known for portraits of Navajo women
- His work blended traditional and modern
- His studio in Taos, NM is now a museum

SHONTO BEGAY (born 1960):
- Contemporary painter
- Known for "Navajo Pop Art" style
- Uses bright colors and geometric forms
- Shows traditional subjects in modern ways

FRANCES LLOYD (1959-2019):
- Master of pictorial weaving
- Wove scenes of Navajo life
- Her work is in the Smithsonian
- Won many awards

WILLIE YAZZIE (born 1963):
- Sandpainter and painter
- Uses traditional sandpainting techniques
- Creates both ceremonial and fine art
- His work is in major museums

MARIA SAMORA (born 1959):
- Multimedia artist
- Works in photography and installation
- Addresses contemporary Navajo issues
- Her work is internationally known

ARTS ORGANIZATIONS:

Navajo Arts and Crafts Enterprise (NACE):
- Promotes authentic Navajo arts
- Certifies genuine Navajo crafts
- Operates retail stores
- Supports artists

Southwest Indian Foundation:
- Supports Navajo artists
- Provides markets for their work
- Offers training programs
- Preserves traditional skills

Gallup Inter-Tribal Ceremonial:
- Annual event since 1922
- Major art competition
- Dance and music performances
- Navajo artists win many awards

Santa Fe Indian Market:
- Largest Native art market in the world
- Held annually in August
- Navajo artists participate
- Prestigious awards

AUTHENTICITY:

How to know if a craft is genuine Navajo:
- Look for the "Navajo Nation Certified" stamp
- Buy from reputable dealers
- Ask about the artist
- Be wary of mass-produced imitations
- Real turquoise has imperfections

The Indian Arts and Crafts Act (1990):
- It is illegal to sell non-Native art as Native-made
- Protects Native artists
- Violations can result in fines or imprisonment
- Supports authentic arts

Sources: Navajo Nation Cultural Resources, Museum of Northern Arizona, Wheelwright Museum, "Navajo Weaving" (Kate Peck Kent), "Navajo Silver" (Marian Rodee).
