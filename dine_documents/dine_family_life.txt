================================================================================
DINÉ FAMILY LIFE: FAMILY STRUCTURE, MARRIAGE, CHILD-REARING, AND DEATH TRADITIONS
================================================================================
Sources: Navajo Nation Cultural Resources, "Navajo Kinship and Marriage" (Gary Witherspoon), "The Navajo" (James F. Downs)


PART 1: THE FAMILY AS THE FOUNDATION OF DINÉ SOCIETY
================================================================================

In Diné culture, the family (K'é) is the most important social unit.

WHAT FAMILY MEANS:

- Not just parents and children
- Includes grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins, and clan relatives
- Extends beyond blood to include community
- Everyone belongs to a family
- No one is alone

Traditional teaching:
"The family is the hogan. The hogan has four poles. The poles are the directions. The directions hold up the sky. The family holds up the people."

FAMILY RESPONSIBILITIES:

- Care for elders
- Raise children
- Support each other in ceremonies
- Share food and resources
- Protect family members
- Teach traditions and language
- Maintain clan relationships

THE EXTENDED FAMILY:

A Diné extended family includes:
- Grandparents (both maternal and paternal)
- Parents
- Children
- Aunts and uncles (both sides)
- Cousins (many)
- In-laws
- Clan relatives (even not blood-related)

Extended families often live near each other:
- Hogans clustered together
- Several related families in one area
- Shared grazing land
- Shared resources
- Mutual support


PART 2: MATRILINEAL CLAN SYSTEM
================================================================================

The Diné clan system is matrilineal (traced through the mother).

WHAT MATRILINEAL MEANS:

- Clan membership comes from the mother
- Children belong to their mother's clan
- The mother's brother (uncle) has authority over children
- Property passes through the female line
- The hogan traditionally belongs to the wife

CLAN RELATIONSHIPS:

Mother's clan (Nishłį́):
- Your primary identity
- Your closest relatives
- You cannot marry within this clan

Father's clan (Bashishchiin):
- Your secondary identity
- "Born for" clan
- You cannot marry within this clan either

Maternal grandfather's clan (Dashicheii):
- Your third identity
- Also restricts marriage

Paternal grandfather's clan (Dashinalí):
- Your fourth identity
- Also restricts marriage

FOUR CLANS:
Every Diné person has four clans:
1. Mother's clan (primary)
2. Father's clan (born for)
3. Maternal grandfather's clan
4. Paternal grandfather's clan

You must know all four clans to properly introduce yourself.


PART 3: ROLES WITHIN THE FAMILY
================================================================================

Each family member has specific roles and responsibilities.

GRANDPARENTS (K'ééháane'é):

Maternal grandmother (Shimásání):
- Most important female elder
- Primary teacher of traditional knowledge
- Teaches weaving, cooking, and child care
- Often the head of the family
- Highly respected

Paternal grandmother (Shinálí):
- Also important
- Teaches different knowledge
- May be less involved than maternal grandmother
- Still highly respected

Maternal grandfather (Shicheii):
- Important male elder
- Teaches hunting, ceremonies, and history
- May be a Hataałii (medicine person)
- Highly respected

Paternal grandfather (Shinálí):
- Also respected
- May teach different skills
- Less involved than maternal grandfather typically

PARENTS:

Mother (Shimá):
- Primary caregiver
- Teaches domestic skills
- Clan identity comes from her
- Children belong to her clan
- Highly respected

Father (Shizhé'é):
- Provider
- Teaches outdoor skills
- His clan is the "born for" clan
- Respected, but mother's brother has more authority

Mother's brother (Shidá'í - maternal uncle):
- Has authority over children
- Disciplines children
- Teaches traditional knowledge
- Sometimes called "male mother"
- Very important figure

Father's sister (Shidá'í - paternal aunt):
- Important female authority
- Teaches special knowledge
- May have role in ceremonies

CHILDREN:

- Learn by observing
- Begin chores at young age (5-6 years old)
- Herding sheep is often first chore
- Respect elders above all
- Obedience is expected
- Curiosity is encouraged (but within limits)

Boys learn from:
- Grandfathers
- Maternal uncles
- Father
- Older brothers

Girls learn from:
- Grandmothers
- Mother
- Maternal aunts
- Older sisters


PART 4: CHILD-REARING (AWÉÉ' BAA NA'AKAAL)
================================================================================

Diné child-rearing practices emphasize respect, observation, and gradual responsibility.

BIRTH AND NAMING:

Traditional birth:
- Baby born at home (in the hogan)
- Assisted by grandmother or midwife
- Father may be present (varies)
- Placenta buried near the hogan (connects baby to land)
- Baby's umbilical cord saved (often attached to cradleboard)

Naming:
- Baby receives first name at birth (or soon after)
- Formal naming may happen later in a ceremony
- Names often describe events, nature, or aspirations
- Some names are sacred (not spoken casually)
- Children may have nicknames used daily

CRADLEBOARD (AWÉÉCH'Í):
- Traditional baby carrier
- Wooden frame with lacing
- Baby wrapped and laced in
- Portable (mother can carry while working)
- Baby feels secure (like in womb)
- Still used today by some families

EARLY CHILDHOOD (0-5 years):

- Baby is carried and held often
- Breastfed for 1-2 years (or longer)
- Toilet training is gentle, not rushed
- Crying is responded to (not ignored)
- Grandmother often primary caregiver

Discipline:
- Gentle guidance
- Distraction (for toddlers)
- No physical punishment (traditional)
- Shaming is avoided
- Praise for good behavior

PRESCHOOL (5-8 years):

Chores begin:
- Herding sheep (most common)
- Gathering wood
- Fetching water
- Helping with cooking
- Watching younger siblings

Learning:
- Observing adults
- Helping with tasks
- Listening to stories
- Learning clan relationships
- Beginning language (if not already fluent)

Discipline:
- Verbal correction
- Loss of privileges
- Being sent to grandmother (for serious issues)
- Still no physical punishment (traditional)

MIDDLE CHILDHOOD (8-12 years):

Chores increase:
- More responsibility for herding
- Cooking (basic)
- Weaving basics (girls)
- Silversmithing basics (boys)
- Farming tasks

Learning:
- More responsibility
- Ceremonial knowledge (appropriate for age)
- Gender-specific skills
- Traditional stories

TEENAGE YEARS (13-18 years):

Chores become adult-like:
- Full responsibility for tasks
- May work for pay (outside family)
- Help with ceremonies

Learning:
- Advanced traditional knowledge
- Courtship and dating
- Clan rules (marriage restrictions)
- Adult responsibilities

Initiation:
- No formal initiation ceremony (traditionally)
- Kinaaldá (girls' puberty ceremony) is important
- Boys may have less formal transition
- Becoming adult is gradual, not sudden

KINAALDÁ (Girls' puberty ceremony):
- Most important ceremony for girls
- Performed at first menstruation
- Lasts 4 days
- Girl runs to the east each morning
- Corn cake baked (symbolic)
- Represents the transformation from girl to woman
- Blessing for fertility and long life
- Still widely practiced today


PART 5: MARRIAGE (ACH'ÍÍ' ÁL'ĮĮ)
================================================================================

Traditional Diné marriage involved clan negotiations and exchange of goods.

MARRIAGE RESTRICTIONS:

You cannot marry anyone in your four clans:
- Your mother's clan
- Your father's clan
- Your maternal grandfather's clan
- Your paternal grandfather's clan

Reason:
- All these people are considered relatives
- Marriage within the clan is incest
- Violations would disrupt the clan system

You CAN marry:
- Someone whose four clans are completely different from yours
- Usually someone from a different area (not your local community)
- Often arranged between families

COURTSHIP:

Traditional courtship:
- Young man visits young woman's family
- He helps with chores (especially herding)
- Relationship is discreet (not public)
- Young man's family approaches young woman's family
- Negotiations begin

Modern courtship:
- Dating (movies, restaurants, etc.)
- Still somewhat discreet
- Family approval important
- Traditional marriage may follow modern dating

MARRIAGE NEGOTIATIONS:

Traditional marriage involved:
- Groom's family visits bride's family
- Gifts exchanged (traditionally sheep, blankets, jewelry)
- Bride price (wealth given to bride's family)
- Agreement on where couple will live

Bride price (Doo ałtah):
- Traditionally paid by groom's family to bride's family
- Sheep, horses, blankets, jewelry
- Compensates bride's family for losing her labor
- Still practiced by some traditional families

WEDDING CEREMONY:

Traditional wedding:
- Performed at the bride's hogan
- Hataałii (medicine person) officiates
- Corn pollen used in blessing
- Corn mush eaten from a single basket (symbolizes unity)
- Water poured (symbolizes life together)
- Couple sits facing east
- Blessing Way songs sung

Traditional wedding basket:
- Tightly woven basket
- Used only for weddings
- Represents the couple's life together
- Corn mush served from the basket
- Kept as a family treasure

The wedding corn mush (Naadą́ą́' baa yilzhóóh):
- Made from white corn (purity)
- Eaten by bride and groom from same basket
- Symbolizes sharing life together
- Also shared with guests

RESIDENCE (Where the couple lives):

Traditionally matrilocal (with wife's family):
- Couple lives near bride's family
- Groom moves to bride's community
- Children grow up near mother's relatives
- Still common today

Sometimes patrilocal (with husband's family):
- Less common
- May happen if bride's family is small
- Or if groom has special circumstances

Neolocal (independent):
- Couple lives separately
- Increasingly common today
- Usually still near one family

MODERN MARRIAGE:

- Many Navajos have civil marriages (justice of the peace)
- Some also have traditional wedding ceremony
- Some have church weddings (Christian)
- Intermarriage with non-Navajo is increasing
- Clan rules still respected by traditional families

DIVORCE:

- Traditionally allowed (not prohibited)
- Simple process: couple separates
- Property divided (woman keeps what she brought)
- Children usually stay with mother's family
- No stigma attached
- Modern legal divorce available


PART 6: FAMILY LIFE CYCLE
================================================================================

Important life events in Diné culture.

BIRTH (Awéé' nidahwiilʼá):

- Baby welcomed into family
- Placenta buried near hogan (connects baby to land)
- Cradleboard used for first months
- Baby named (often after a few days)
- No formal baptism (traditional)
- Christian baptism for Christian families

PUBERTY (Girls - Kinaaldá):

- Most important ceremony for girls
- 4 days of ceremonies
- Girl runs to the east each morning
- Corn cake baked
- Girl becomes woman

PUBERTY (Boys):

- No formal ceremony (traditionally)
- Some boys may have ceremonies if they have special visions
- Boys transition gradually through responsibilities

MARRIAGE (Ach'íí' áł'įį):

- Couple formally united
- Ceremony may be traditional, civil, or church
- New family established
- Clan relationships extended

PARENTHOOD:

- Children bring status
- Large families valued (traditionally)
- Children cared for by extended family
- Grandparents actively involved
- Parents respected for raising children

ELDERHOOD (K'ééháane'é):

- Elders are most respected
- They hold traditional knowledge
- They are caregivers for grandchildren
- They are consulted on family decisions
- They are never placed in nursing homes (traditionally)

DEATH (Hózhǫ́ǫ́gi át'é):

See Part 8 for detailed death traditions


PART 7: EXTENDED FAMILY RELATIONSHIPS
================================================================================

Diné kinship terms reflect the importance of extended family.

RELATIONSHIP TERMS:

Parents:
- Shimá - My mother
- Shizhé'é - My father

Grandparents:
- Shimásání - My maternal grandmother
- Shinálí - My paternal grandmother
- Shicheii - My maternal grandfather
- Shinálí - My paternal grandfather

Aunts:
- Shibízhí - My maternal aunt
- Shidá'í - My paternal aunt

Uncles:
- Shidá'í - My maternal uncle
- Shizhé'é - My paternal uncle

Siblings:
- Shidine'é - My older sister
- Shitsilí - My younger sister
- Shinááí - My older brother
- Shitsilí - My younger brother

Cousins:
- Shik'is - My sibling/cousin (parallel cousin, same clan - not marriageable)
- Shiláán - My cross-cousin (different clan - marriageable)

Children:
- Shiye' - My son
- Shi'áád - My daughter

Grandchildren:
- Shiyáázh - My grandchild

CALLING RELATIVES:

- Kinship terms are used as names
- You call your grandmother "Shimásání" (not her name)
- You call your uncle "Shidá'í" (not his name)
- Shows respect
- Maintains family connections

K'é RELATIONSHIPS:

K'é extends beyond blood:
- Adopted children are called "my child"
- Close family friends may be called "aunt" or "uncle"
- Clan relatives (not blood-related) are called by kinship terms
- Everyone is related through k'é


PART 8: DEATH AND MOURNING (HOZHǪ́Ǫ́GI ÁT'É)
================================================================================

Diné death traditions are complex and often not discussed openly.

TRADITIONAL BELIEFS ABOUT DEATH:

- Death is a natural part of life
- The spirit (soul) continues after death
- Ghosts (ch'į́į́dii) can harm the living if not properly managed
- The dead must be treated with respect and caution
- Death disrupts Hózhó (harmony) and must be restored

THE CAUSE OF DEATH:

Traditionally, death was caused by:
- Witchcraft (some cases)
- Violation of taboos
- Enemy attack
- Accident
- Old age (natural, least problematic)

THE BODY AFTER DEATH:

Traditional practices:
- Body washed and dressed in best clothes
- Hair fixed (for women, Tsiiyéél)
- Body placed in traditional burial position (flexed, facing east)
- Body buried quickly (within 24 hours)
- Burial before dawn or at dawn

Burial location:
- Away from the hogan (not near living areas)
- In a secluded place
- Not marked (to prevent ghost disturbances)
- No permanent grave markers (traditionally)

MOURNING:

Traditional mourning practices:
- Family members stay inside (not working) for 4 days
- Do not bathe or wash hair for 4 days
- Do not cook for others (others cook for the family)
- Do not visit others (others may visit them)
- Do not attend ceremonies for 4 days to a year
- Do not say the deceased person's name (for a period)

The 4-day period:
- Four is the sacred number
- The spirit remains near for 4 days
- After 4 days, the spirit departs
- Normal activities resume gradually

NAMING THE DECEASED:

- The deceased person's name is not spoken for a period of time
- Avoid saying the name (may attract the ghost)
- Refer to them as "the one who died" or by relationship
- Eventually, the name may be spoken again (after a year or more)
- Some names are never spoken again

BELONGINGS OF THE DECEASED:

- Some belongings may be buried with the body
- Other belongings may be destroyed
- Or given away (to non-family members)
- Keeping belongings may attract the ghost
- Clothing is often destroyed

THE HOGAN AFTER DEATH:

- If someone dies in the hogan, the hogan may be abandoned
- Or the hogan may be ritually purified
- Traditional: burn the hogan (if death was by witchcraft)
- Modern: hogan may be torn down
- Some families continue using the hogan after purification

CHRISTIAN INFLUENCE:

- Many Navajos are Christian
- Christian burial practices are common
- Funeral services at churches
- Cemeteries with marked graves
- Traditional and Christian practices sometimes combined

GHOSTS (CH'Į́Į́DII):

Beliefs about ghosts:
- Ghosts can harm the living
- Ghosts can cause illness
- Ghosts are attracted to those who speak of the dead
- Ghosts linger near burial places
- Avoid burial areas, especially at night

Protection from ghosts:
- Corn pollen (offerings)
- Prayers
- Avoid speaking of the dead
- Stay away from burial areas
- Ceremonies to remove ghost sickness (Ch'į́į́dii bits'ą́ą́')


PART 9: FAMILY GATHERINGS AND FEASTS
================================================================================

Family gatherings are important for maintaining K'é.

TYPES OF GATHERINGS:

Ceremonial feasts:
- After a ceremony (Blessing Way, Night Way, etc.)
- Extended family gathers
- Food shared
- Thanks given

Holiday gatherings:
- Thanksgiving
- Christmas (many Navajos are Christian)
- New Year's
- Easter

Family celebrations:
- Birthdays
- Graduations
- Weddings
- Kinaaldá (girls' puberty ceremony)

SHEEP HERDING GATHERINGS:

- Families gather to herd sheep
- Lambing season (spring)
- Shearing (spring)
- Working together
- Food shared

FOOD AT GATHERINGS:

Traditional foods:
- Mutton stew (most common)
- Frybread
- Blue corn mush
- Roasted mutton
- Coffee

Modern additions:
- Fried chicken
- Potluck dishes
- Sodas
- Store-bought desserts

GIFT GIVING:

- Gifts often exchanged at gatherings
- Traditionally practical gifts (food, blankets, jewelry)
- Modern gifts (store-bought items)
- Gift giving strengthens K'é
- Reciprocity expected (gift in return later)


PART 10: FAMILY IN MODERN TIMES
================================================================================

Diné families face challenges today.

CHALLENGES:

- Boarding school legacy (separated families)
- Unemployment and poverty
- Substance abuse
- Domestic violence
- Elder care (adults work away from home)
- Child care (grandparents often raise grandchildren)
- Language loss (younger generations not fluent)

STRENGTHS:

- Strong family bonds
- Extended family support
- K'é remains central
- Clan connections maintained
- Traditional values persist
- Grandparents involved in raising grandchildren
- Families help each other

FAMILY TRENDS:

- More nuclear families (living apart from extended family)
- Intermarriage increasing
- Some families maintain traditional practices
- Others are more modern
- Many blend traditional and modern

GRANDPARENTS RAISING GRANDCHILDREN:

- Common due to parents working or struggling
- Grandparents often primary caregivers
- Traditional: grandparents are teachers
- Modern: grandparents are parents again
- Challenges: grandparents may be elderly, have health issues

K'É TODAY:

- Still the foundation of Diné society
- Still taught to children
- Still practiced at ceremonies
- Still shapes how Navajos relate to each other
- Adapting to modern life


PART 11: FAMILY LIFE PHRASES
================================================================================

VOCABULARY:

- K'é - Kinship / family / relationships
- K'ééháane'é - Elders (respected family members)
- Awéé' - Baby / infant
- Ałchíní - Children
- Nidaʼałchíní - Offspring / descendants
- K'éí - Relatives
- Bíighah - Clan relative
- Hózhǫ́ǫ́gi át'é - Death (literally "he/she became beauty" - euphemism)

PHRASES:

- ShiK'é - My family / my relatives
- Shimásání - My maternal grandmother
- Shinálí - My paternal grandmother / grandfather
- Shicheii - My maternal grandfather
- Shimá - My mother
- Shizhé'é - My father
- Shidine'é - My older sister
- Shinááí - My older brother
- Shitsilí - My younger sibling
- Shiye' - My son
- Shi'áád - My daughter

FAMILY CONVERSATION:

- Haash éí binilii? - What is your clan?
- [Clan] nishłį́ - I am [Clan]
- [Clan] bashishchiin - Born for [Clan]
- Dóoneʼé nishłį́? - What clan are you?
- NihiK'é haashę́ę? - How is your family?
- NihiK'é nizhóní - My family is good / beautiful
- NihiK'é baa nitsíjíkees - I am thinking of my family
- NihiK'é yá'át'ééh - My family is good

RESPECTFUL ADDRESS:

- Shimásání - Grandmother (maternal)
- Shinálí - Grandmother/ (paternal)
- Shicheii - Grandfather (maternal)
- Shimá - Mother
- Shizhé'é - Father
- Shidá'í - Aunt / Uncle
- Shibízhí - Aunt (maternal)
- Shidine'é - Older sister (respect)
- Shinááí - Older brother (respect)
