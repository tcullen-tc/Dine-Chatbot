================================================================================
DINÉ SONGS & MUSIC: TRADITIONAL CHANTS, INSTRUMENTS, AND DANCE
================================================================================
Sources: Navajo Nation Cultural Resources, Museum of Northern Arizona, "Navajo Music" (David McAllester)


PART 1: THE SACRED ROLE OF SONG IN DINÉ CULTURE
================================================================================

In Diné culture, songs are prayers. Every ceremony, every healing, every important event includes songs.

WHY SONGS ARE SACRED:

- The Holy People (Diyin Dineʼé) gave songs to the Diné
- Songs are how prayers are delivered
- Each song has specific power and purpose
- Songs must be sung correctly (mistakes have consequences)
- The Holy People hear songs and respond

According to tradition:
"The Holy People gave us songs. With songs, we pray. With songs, we heal. With songs, we remember. The songs are our connection to the Holy People."

THE POWER OF SONGS:

- Healing songs can cure illness
- Protection songs can ward off danger
- Blessing songs bring good fortune
- Ceremonial songs invoke the Holy People
- Everyday songs give thanks

SONGS AND HÓZHÓ:

Songs are central to restoring Hózhó (harmony/balance):
- When a person is ill, songs are sung to restore balance
- Each ceremony has hundreds of songs
- The songs call the Holy People to help
- The songs create a path for healing


PART 2: TYPES OF CEREMONIAL SONGS
================================================================================

BLESSING WAY SONGS (Hózhóójí):

- Performed during the Blessing Way ceremony
- Called the "beauty way" songs
- Attract good, health, and happiness
- Used for blessing new homes, marriages, babies
- Considered the most positive songs

Key features:
- Upbeat, hopeful melodies
- Words focus on beauty and harmony
- Sung during daylight hours
- End with "Hózhó" (beauty/harmony)

Example lyrics (translated):
"In beauty I walk
With beauty before me, I walk
With beauty behind me, I walk
With beauty above me, I walk
With beauty below me, I walk
With beauty all around me, I walk
In old age, walking in beauty
With beauty, it is finished."

NIGHT WAY SONGS (Tł'éé'jí):

- Performed during the Night Way ceremony
- 9 nights of songs (over 300 individual songs)
- Used for healing serious illness
- Sung at night (as the name suggests)
- Involves masked dancers (Yé'iibicheii)

Key features:
- Complex, lengthy songs
- Sung by trained chanters
- Each night has specific songs
- The final night includes all-night singing

ENEMY WAY SONGS (Anaa'í):

- Performed during the Enemy Way ceremony
- Used to treat illness from contact with outsiders
- Also used for veterans returning from war
- Removes "enemy" contamination

Key features:
- Songs about enemies and warfare
- Includes dance songs
- Ends with a public dance

MOUNTAIN WAY SONGS (Tó Díínííl):

- Performed during the Mountain Way ceremony
- Used for bear-related illness
- Also for injuries from falls
- Includes mountain-themed songs

Key features:
- Songs about bears and mountains
- Deep, powerful melodies
- Sung by specialized chanters

HORSE WAY SONGS (Łį́į́ Naat'ááh):

- Performed during the Horse Way ceremony
- Used for horse-related illness
- Also to bless horses
- Songs about horses and riding

Key features:
- Imitate horse sounds
- Rhythmic, galloping melodies
- Sung for both healing and blessing


PART 3: THE STRUCTURE OF DINÉ SONGS
================================================================================

Diné songs have specific structures that must be followed.

TYPICAL SONG STRUCTURE:

Each song has four parts (the sacred number four):

1. Opening phrase (introduction)
2. First repetition (first verse)
3. Second repetition (second verse - often identical to first)
4. Closing phrase (conclusion)

Example structure:
- Introduction (4 lines)
- Verse 1 (4 lines)
- Verse 2 (4 lines)
- Conclusion (4 lines)

Total: 16 lines (4 x 4 = 16, the sacred number)

MUSICAL ELEMENTS:

Melody:
- Usually within a narrow range (5-7 notes)
- Repetitive patterns
- Descending phrases common
- No harmony (solo voice)

Rhythm:
- Free rhythm (not strict meter)
- Sometimes steady pulse (for dance songs)
- Rhythmic patterns repeat

Dynamics:
- Generally consistent volume
- Sometimes swelling for emphasis
- No sudden changes

TEXTURE:
- Solo voice (most common)
- Call and response (some ceremonies)
- Unison singing (rare)

LENGTH:
- Most songs last 1-3 minutes
- Some ceremonial songs last much longer
- Night Way songs can be 10+ minutes


PART 4: THE ROLE OF THE CHANTER (HATAAŁII)
================================================================================

The Hataałii (medicine person/chants) is the song leader and ceremonial expert.

RESPONSIBILITIES OF THE HATAAŁII:

- Memorize hundreds of songs
- Sing the songs correctly (mistakes have consequences)
- Lead the ceremony
- Train apprentices
- Preserve sacred knowledge

TRAINING:

- Must be called by the Holy People (often through dreams)
- Apprentice with an experienced Hataałii for 10-20 years
- Learn songs, prayers, sandpaintings, and herbs
- Must memorize perfectly (no written music)
- Initiation ceremony

WHAT A HATAAŁII MUST MEMORIZE:

- Blessing Way: approximately 100-200 songs
- Night Way: over 300 songs
- Other ceremonies: hundreds more
- Total repertoire: 500-1,000+ songs
- All without written notation

QUOTE FROM A HATAAŁII:
"The songs are not written down. They are in my heart. The Holy People put them there. When I sing, the Holy People hear. When I make a mistake, they are angry. So I must sing perfectly."


PART 5: TRADITIONAL DINÉ INSTRUMENTS
================================================================================

Diné music traditionally uses only percussion and voice (no melody instruments except voice).

DRUM (ÁSAA'):

Types of drums:
- Water drum (filled with water)
- Frame drum (animal hide stretched over frame)
- Pottery drum (clay vessel with hide cover)

Water drum (most sacred):
- Made from a pottery vessel or metal kettle
- Filled partially with water
- Hide stretched over the top
- Water changes the pitch
- Used in Night Way ceremony

Frame drum:
- Wooden frame with hide stretched over
- No water
- Used in various ceremonies
- Portable

Drum stick (Béésh):
- Wooden stick
- Sometimes wrapped with cloth
- Strikes the drum head

RATTLE (ADEE'):

Types of rattles:
- Gourd rattle (most common)
- Turtle shell rattle (ceremonial)
- Tin can rattle (modern substitute)

Gourd rattle (Adee'):
- Made from dried gourd
- Filled with seeds or small stones
- Handle is the gourd stem
- Used in many ceremonies

Turtle shell rattle:
- Made from turtle shell
- Filled with stones
- Used in specific ceremonies
- Associated with water

Rattle sounds:
- Represents the sound of rain
- Calls the Holy People
- Keeps rhythm for songs
- Clears negative energy

BULLROARER (NDAAS'ÍNÍ):

- Wooden slat on a string
- Whirled in the air to produce a roaring sound
- Used in ceremonies (especially Night Way)
- Represents the voice of the wind
- Not for public display

FLUTE (TSIIZÍ):

- Wooden flute (traditional)
- Rare in traditional Diné music
- More common in modern Diné music
- Used for personal expression (not ceremony)

WHISTLE:
- Made from bone or wood
- Used in specific ceremonies
- Imitates bird calls
- Not common


PART 6: DANCE (NDAA')
================================================================================

Dance is an important part of Diné ceremonies.

CEREMONIAL DANCE:

Yé'iibicheii dance (Night Way):
- Masked dancers represent Holy People
- Performed during Night Way ceremony
- All-night dancing on the final night
- Dancers are men (traditionally)

SQUAW DANCE (public dance after Enemy Way):
- Social dance (not ceremonial)
- Women choose dance partners
- Men pay women to dance
- Fundraiser for the ceremony

FIRE DANCE:
- Part of the Mountain Way ceremony
- Dancers handle fire
- Represents the power of fire
- Dangerous, requires skill

BASKET DANCE:
- Women dance around a ceremonial basket
- Part of wedding ceremonies
- Represents the circle of life
- Women sing while dancing

TRADITIONAL DANCE STYLES:

- Steps are simple (stamping, shuffling)
- No leaps or turns
- Dancers move in a circle
- Direction is clockwise (following the sun)
- Group dances are common


PART 7: MODERN DINÉ MUSIC
================================================================================

Contemporary Diné musicians blend traditional and modern styles.

TRADITIONAL SINGERS TODAY:

- Continue to perform ceremonial songs
- Record traditional songs (with permission)
- Teach younger generations
- Perform at powwows and ceremonies

Examples:
- Navajo Nation singing groups

PART 8: EVERYDAY AND SOCIAL SONGS
================================================================================

Not all Diné songs are ceremonial.

LULLABIES (Awééch'í):

- Sung to babies and children
- Soothing melodies
- Simple words
- Often improvised
- Passed from grandmothers to grandchildren

Example lullaby (translated):
"Sleep, my baby, sleep
The sheep are sleeping
The horses are sleeping
The stars are watching over you"

WORK SONGS:

- Sung while weaving
- Sung while herding sheep
- Sung while farming
- Make work easier
- Often improvised

WEAVING SONGS:
- Sung while at the loom
- Some songs are prayers
- Others are just for enjoyment
- Passed from mother to daughter

HERDING SONGS:
- Sung while tending sheep
- Call to the sheep
- Imitate sheep sounds
- Keep sheep together

HORSE SONGS:
- Sung while riding
- Calm the horse
- Express joy of riding
- Used in Horse Way ceremony

LOVE SONGS:

- Not traditional (public expressions of love were not common)
- Modern love songs exist
- Often in English (or bilingual)
- Popular at social gatherings

HUMOROUS SONGS:

- Comic songs exist
- Often about Coyote (Ma'ii)
- Used to teach lessons through laughter
- Usually not ceremonial


PART 9: SONG PROTOCOL AND ETIQUETTE
================================================================================

Proper behavior when songs are sung:

DURING CEREMONIAL SONGS:

- Be quiet and respectful
- Do not sing along (unless invited)
- Do not record without permission
- Do not clap or cheer
- Sit still (do not walk around)
- Remove hats
- No photography

DURING SOCIAL SONGS:

- Clapping is allowed
- Singing along is welcome
- Dancing may be appropriate
- Still be respectful
- Follow the lead of elders

WHO CAN SING CERTAIN SONGS:

- Ceremonial songs: only trained Hataałii
- Everyday songs: anyone can sing
- Sacred songs: never for entertainment
- Family songs: within the family
- Public songs: at appropriate events

WHEN SONGS CAN BE SUNG:

- Ceremonial songs: only during ceremonies
- Winter stories songs: only in winter (November-March)
- Night Way songs: only at night
- Blessing songs: anytime (but best in morning)

RECORDING AND SHARING SONGS:

- Do not record ceremonial songs without permission
- Some songs are too sacred to share
- Commercial use requires permission
- Always credit the source
- Respect cultural ownership


PART 10: THE NAVAJO NATION'S SONG HERITAGE
================================================================================

The Navajo Nation protects its song heritage.

NAVAJO NATION CULTURAL RESOURCES:

- Navajo Nation Historic Preservation Department
- Navajo Nation Museum
- Navajo Nation Library
- Navajo Nation Archives

These institutions preserve:
- Recordings of traditional songs
- Documentation of song protocols
- Histories of ceremonies
- Biographies of Hataałii

SONG PRESERVATION EFFORTS:

- Recording traditional singers (with permission)
- Archiving at Navajo Nation Museum
- Teaching songs to young people
- Language immersion includes songs
- Hataałii training programs

CHALLENGES:

- Fewer young people learning ceremonial songs
- Hataałii are aging
- Some songs have been lost
- Language loss affects song understanding
- Cultural appropriation (outsiders using songs without permission)

HOW YOU CAN HELP:

- Do not appropriate Diné songs
- Support Navajo Nation cultural programs
- Attend public events respectfully
- Encourage authentic preservation
- Respect song protocols


PART 11: COMMON SONGS AND THEIR MEANINGS
================================================================================

THE HÓZHÓOGO NAASHÁ SONG (Walking in Beauty):

This is the most famous Diné song/prayer.

Text (Navajo):
"Hózhóogo naashá
Hózhóogo naashá
Shitsʼil iiná
Hózhóogo naashá
Hózhóogo naashá
Hózhóogo naashá
Hózhóogo naashá"

Translation:
"In beauty I walk
In beauty I walk
With long life
In beauty I walk
In beauty I walk
In beauty I walk
In beauty I walk"

THE BLESSING WAY OPENING SONG:

Used to begin the Blessing Way ceremony.

"Yá'át'ééh nizhóní
Hózhó náhásdlį́į́
Tádídín bee shá
Nizhóní
Hózhó"

Translation:
"It is good, it is beautiful
Harmony is restored
With corn pollen
It is beautiful
Harmony"

THE NIGHT WAY CLOSING SONG:

Sung at dawn on the final night of Night Way.

"Náhookǫs biʼdookʼǫ́ǫ́
Sisnaajiní bikááʼ
Hózhóogo naashá
Nizhóní"

Translation:
"Northern lights
On Blanca Peak
I walk in beauty
It is beautiful"

THE CORN GRINDING SONG:

Sung while grinding corn (traditional women's work).

"Naadą́ą́' deeshją́
Shizhé'é deeshją́
Shimá deeshją́
Naadą́ą́' deeshją́"

Translation:
"I grind the corn
For my father, I grind
For my mother, I grind
I grind the corn"


PART 12: DINÉ MUSIC PHRASES
================================================================================

VOCABULARY:

- Sin - Song
- Hataałii - Chanter / medicine person
- Ndaa' - Dance
- Ásaa' - Drum
- Adee' - Rattle
- Tsii zí - Flute
- Ndilnish - Ceremony (general)
- Yá'át'ééh - Good/beautiful (often in songs)
- Nizhóní - Beautiful (often in songs)
- Hózhó - Harmony/beauty (central to songs)

PHRASES:

- Sin yishłé - I am singing
- Sin yisht'įįh - I hear a song
- Sin yá'át'ééh - The song is good
- Sin nizhóní - The song is beautiful
- Hataałii sin yilé - The medicine person sings
- Sin bá hózhǫ́ - The song is good/beautiful

QUESTIONS ABOUT SONGS:

- Haash sin? - What song?
- Háí sin yilé? - Who is singing?
- Hádą́ sin? - When is the song?
- Háadi sin? - Where is the song?

RESPECTFUL LISTENING:

- Sin yá'át'ééh - The song is good (to compliment)
- Sin nizhóní - The song is beautiful
- Ahé'hee sin baa - Thank you for the song
- Sin yisht'įįh - I hear the song (acknowledgment)

Sources: Navajo Nation Cultural Resources, Museum of Northern Arizona, "Navajo Music" (David McAllester), "The Blessing Way" (Leland C. Wyman).
