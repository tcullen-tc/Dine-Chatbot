================================================================================
DINÉ EDUCATION: TRADITIONAL LEARNING, BOARDING SCHOOLS, DINÉ COLLEGE, AND TODAY
================================================================================
Sources: Navajo Nation Department of Diné Education, Diné College, National Native American Boarding School Coalition


PART 1: TRADITIONAL DINÉ EDUCATION
================================================================================

Before European contact, Diné children learned through observation, participation, and oral tradition.

HOW TRADITIONAL EDUCATION WORKED:

Learning by observing (Nitsáhákees):
- Children watched adults perform daily tasks
- They listened to elders tell stories
- They learned without being directly taught
- Questions were encouraged after observation

Learning by doing (Iiná):
- Children helped with age-appropriate tasks
- Girls learned weaving, cooking, and farming
- Boys learned hunting, herding, and ceremonies
- Mistakes were corrected gently

Learning from family (K'é):
- Grandparents were primary teachers
- Clan relatives shared specialized knowledge
- Each family member had teaching responsibilities
- Learning was a community effort

THE ROLE OF ELDERS (K'ééháane'é):
- Elders were the most respected teachers
- They held traditional knowledge
- They taught through stories and examples
- They modeled proper behavior

THE ROLE OF STORIES (Hááne'é):
- Stories taught moral lessons
- Winter stories (Hááne'é) taught creation and history
- Coyote stories taught consequences of bad behavior
- Hero Twins stories taught courage and perseverance

WHAT WAS TAUGHT:

Practical skills:
- Weaving, silversmithing, pottery
- Farming (corn, beans, squash)
- Sheep herding and animal care
- Hunting and gathering
- Food preparation and storage

Cultural knowledge:
- Clan relationships (K'é)
- Proper introductions
- Ceremonial knowledge
- Songs and prayers
- Sacred places

Values:
- Respect for elders (Hodzil)
- Generosity (Áłah nílʼįįh)
- Responsibility (K'éí dóó áháyá)
- Harmony (Hózhó)
- Perseverance

Spiritual education:
- Relationship with Holy People (Diyin Dineʼé)
- Prayer and offerings
- Ceremonial protocols
- Healing knowledge
- Proper behavior in sacred spaces


PART 2: THE BOARDING SCHOOL ERA (1880-1960)
================================================================================

The U.S. government established boarding schools to forcibly assimilate Native American children.

THE POLICY:

Purpose of boarding schools:
- "Kill the Indian, save the man" (Captain Richard Pratt, Carlisle School)
- Erase Native languages and cultures
- Force Christian conversion
- Teach English and European ways
- Prepare Native children for manual labor

Richard Henry Pratt's statement (1892):
"A great general has said that the only good Indian is a dead one. In a sense, I agree with the sentiment, but only in this: that all the Indian there is in the race should be dead. Kill the Indian in him, and save the man."

BOARDING SCHOOLS FOR NAVAJO CHILDREN:

Major schools that Navajo children attended:
- Carlisle Indian Industrial School (Pennsylvania)
- Haskell Institute (Kansas)
- Sherman Institute (California)
- Albuquerque Indian School (New Mexico)
- Fort Defiance Boarding School (Arizona - on Navajo Nation)
- Many others across the country

THE EXPERIENCE:

Forced removal:
- Children were taken from their families (sometimes forcibly)
- Parents who resisted could be jailed
- Children were sent far from home (often hundreds or thousands of miles)
- Siblings were often separated

Conditions at the schools:
- Overcrowded dormitories
- Poor nutrition and medical care
- Physical and emotional abuse was common
- Disease spread quickly
- Many children died (estimated 20-40% mortality rate at some schools)

Forbidden practices:
- Speaking Navajo was strictly forbidden (punished with physical abuse)
- Traditional clothing was confiscated
- Traditional haircuts were forced (long hair was cut)
- Navajo names were replaced with English numbers or names
- Traditional ceremonies and prayers were banned

Punishments:
- Beating for speaking Navajo
- Solitary confinement
- Withholding food
- Manual labor (often hard physical work)
- Public humiliation

THE LASTING TRAUMA:

Effects on boarding school survivors:
- Loss of Navajo language (many lost fluency)
- Loss of cultural knowledge
- Difficulty parenting (never learned from their own parents)
- Physical and emotional scars
- Intergenerational trauma passed to children

Effects on Navajo communities:
- Broken family bonds
- Loss of traditional knowledge
- Language decline
- Cultural disconnection
- Suspicion of schools and authority

The Navajo Nation estimates:
- Nearly every Navajo family was affected by boarding schools
- Thousands of children attended
- Many never returned home (died at school)
- The trauma continues to affect generations

Survivor testimony:
"They cut my hair. They gave me a number. They said my name was 247. I cried for my mother. They beat me for speaking Navajo. I never spoke Navajo again." - Anonymous boarding school survivor

FEDERAL APOLOGY (2009):
- The U.S. government formally apologized for boarding school policies
- The apology acknowledged the harm caused
- No financial compensation was provided
- Many survivors felt the apology was insufficient

Interior Secretary Deb Haaland (2021):
"These boarding schools were designed to erase Native American cultures. The trauma continues to this day. We must acknowledge this history and work to heal our communities."


PART 3: THE ROUGH ROCK DEMONSTRATION SCHOOL (1966)
================================================================================

The Rough Rock Demonstration School was a turning point for Navajo education.

BACKGROUND:

Before Rough Rock:
- Navajo children attended BIA (Bureau of Indian Affairs) schools
- Curriculum was designed by non-Navajo administrators
- Navajo language and culture were not taught
- Academic outcomes were poor
- Dropout rates were high

THE VISION (1966):
- First school completely controlled by a Navajo school board
- Located in Rough Rock, Arizona (on Navajo Nation)
- Navajo language and culture were central to curriculum
- Parents and community members were involved
- The school was designed BY Navajo FOR Navajo

KEY INNOVATIONS:

Navajo language instruction:
- Classes taught in Navajo language
- Navajo was used in hallways and common areas
- Language immersion approach
- Elders served as language teachers

Cultural curriculum:
- Navajo history taught from Navajo perspective
- Traditional stories (Hááne'é) were taught
- Clan system was explained
- Ceremonial knowledge (appropriate for school) was shared

Community involvement:
- Navajo school board (first of its kind)
- Parents welcomed in classrooms
- Elders served as advisors and teachers
- Community meetings to plan the school

Academic results:
- Improved attendance (students wanted to come)
- Higher academic achievement
- Navajo language retention
- Graduates were prepared for both Navajo and mainstream worlds

LEGACY OF ROUGH ROCK:
- Inspired other tribal schools across the country
- Model for Navajo Nation schools today
- Proved that Native-controlled schools work
- Launched Navajo bilingual education
- Still operating today (Rough Rock Community School)


PART 4: DINÉ COLLEGE (TSÉGHÓÓDIʼOÓŁTĄ́ NÍLÉÉH)
================================================================================

Diné College was the first tribal college in the United States.

FOUNDING (1968):

When established: 1968
Where: Tsaile, Arizona (on Navajo Nation)
Why: To provide higher education on the reservation
Founder: Dr. Robert Roessel (with Navajo Nation leaders)

First of its kind:
- First college owned and operated by a Native American tribe
- Model for all subsequent tribal colleges
- Serves Navajo Nation and surrounding communities
- Now part of the 35+ tribal college system

THE CAMPUS:

Main campus: Tsaile, Arizona
Other locations:
- Shiprock, New Mexico
- Crownpoint, New Mexico
- Chinle, Arizona
- Window Rock, Arizona
- Online programs

Facilities:
- Hogan-shaped buildings (traditional architecture)
- Library and learning center
- Science labs
- Art studios (weaving, silversmithing)
- Student housing

THE MISSION:

Diné College mission statement:
"To provide quality postsecondary education that is rooted in Diné culture and language, preparing students to become leaders in their communities."

Core values:
- Sa'ah Naagháí Bik'eh Hózhóón (SNBH) - the Diné philosophy
- K'é - kinship and relationships
- Diné language preservation
- Community service
- Leadership development

ACADEMIC PROGRAMS:

Associate degrees:
- Diné Studies (language, culture, history)
- Navajo Language
- Native American Studies
- Business
- Education
- Nursing (pre-nursing)
- Criminal Justice
- Early Childhood Education

Bachelor's degrees:
- Diné Education (teacher training)
- Navajo Language
- Diné Studies (selected topics)
- Business Administration
- Psychology

Certificate programs:
- Navajo Language (beginning to advanced)
- Diné Culture
- Traditional Arts (weaving, silversmithing)
- Native Plant Medicine

STUDENT LIFE:

Enrollment: Approximately 1,500-2,000 students per semester
Student demographics: ~95% Navajo
Average age: 27 (many non-traditional students)
Student services:
- Tutoring and academic support
- Childcare for student parents
- Counseling services
- Career placement

Traditional activities:
- Weekly Navajo language tables
- Traditional arts workshops
- Ceremonial knowledge (appropriate for school)
- Clan gatherings
- Pow wow participation

NOTABLE ALUMNI:
- Many Navajo Nation leaders (presidents, council members)
- Teachers in Navajo schools
- Nurses on the reservation
- Artists and weavers
- Language preservationists


PART 5: OTHER NAVAJO EDUCATIONAL INSTITUTIONS
================================================================================

NAVAJO TECHNICAL UNIVERSITY (NTU):

Location: Crownpoint, New Mexico
Founded: 1979 (as Navajo Skill Center)
Type: Technical and vocational education

Programs:
- Construction technology
- Automotive technology
- Computer information systems
- Business management
- Early childhood education
- Nursing
- Welding

Unique programs:
- Diné culture and language
- Traditional arts
- Sustainable energy
- Environmental science
- Agriculture

Enrollment: Approximately 1,500 students

NAVAJO NATION DEPARTMENT OF DINÉ EDUCATION (NDDE):

Purpose: Oversees all Navajo education programs
Location: Window Rock, Arizona

Responsibilities:
- Sets educational standards for Navajo schools
- Develops Diné language and culture curriculum
- Trains Navajo teachers
- Administers scholarship programs
- Supports early childhood education

THE OFFICE OF NAVAJO HEAD START:
- Early childhood education for Navajo children
- Serves over 3,000 children annually
- 70+ centers across Navajo Nation
- Navajo language and culture integrated into curriculum
- Family support services


PART 6: CURRENT EDUCATIONAL CHALLENGES
================================================================================

Navajo education today faces significant challenges.

LANGUAGE LOSS:
- Only 148,000 fluent Navajo speakers remain
- Most are over age 50
- Few children are learning Navajo at home
- Language immersion programs are growing but insufficient
- Loss of language means loss of culture

ACHIEVEMENT GAP:
- Navajo students lag behind national averages in reading and math
- High school graduation rates: ~70% (below national average)
- College enrollment: lower than national average
- College completion: significant challenges

FUNDING ISSUES:
- Tribal schools are chronically underfunded
- BIA funding formula is outdated
- Facilities are aging and in disrepair
- Teacher salaries are lower than surrounding districts
- Many schools lack basic resources (books, technology, heat)

INFRASTRUCTURE:
- Many schools lack reliable internet
- Some lack running water or electricity
- Bus transportation is challenging (large distances)
- Winter weather closes roads and schools
- COVID-19 revealed many infrastructure gaps

HEALTH AND SOCIAL ISSUES:
- Poverty affects many Navajo families
- Food insecurity impacts student learning
- Housing shortages mean crowded conditions
- Mental health services are limited
- Substance abuse affects some families

GEOGRAPHY:
- Navajo Nation is 27,000 square miles
- Students travel long distances to school
- Some bus rides are 2-3 hours each way
- Boarding schools are still used for remote areas
- Homeschooling is common in some areas


PART 7: EDUCATIONAL SUCCESS STORIES
================================================================================

Despite challenges, many Navajo education programs succeed.

DINÉ LANGUAGE IMMERSION PROGRAMS:

Tséhootsooí Diné Language Immersion School (Fort Defiance, AZ):
- Pre-K through 6th grade
- All instruction in Navajo (except English class)
- Students become fluent in Navajo
- Academic achievement meets or exceeds English-only schools
- Model for other immersion programs

Tse'bii'nidzisgai Elementary School (Tuba City, AZ):
- Navajo language immersion
- Cultural integration
- Parent involvement
- Improved attendance and achievement

NÍHIZAADÍÍ (Navajo Language Immersion Program):
- Public school option in several districts
- After-school and summer programs
- Adult language classes
- Teacher training

COLLEGE AND CAREER SUCCESS:

Navajo Nation Scholarship Office:
- Provides scholarships for Navajo students
- Over 3,000 students receive aid annually
- Priority to students pursuing degrees needed on the reservation
- Many graduates return to work for Navajo Nation

The American Indian College Fund:
- Supports Navajo students at tribal colleges
- Provides scholarships and mentoring
- Graduation rates higher than national Native average
- Many graduates become teachers and nurses

CAREER SUCCESS STORIES:
- Navajo doctors, lawyers, and engineers (still too few, but growing)
- Navajo teachers (increasing numbers)
- Navajo nurses (serving reservation hospitals)
- Navajo entrepreneurs (starting businesses)
- Navajo artists (internationally recognized)


PART 8: INDIVIDUALIZED EDUCATION PROGRAMS (IEP)
================================================================================

What is an IEP?:
- A plan for students with disabilities
- Created by parents, teachers, and specialists
- Ensures appropriate accommodations
- Legally required by IDEA (Individuals with Disabilities Education Act)

How IEPs work on Navajo Nation:
- Same legal requirements as everywhere in U.S.
- Challenges: fewer specialists available
- Remote locations make services difficult
- Telehealth is improving access
- Parent advocacy is crucial

Cultural considerations for IEPs:
- Some disabilities are understood through Diné worldview
- Family (K'é) involvement is central
- Elders may provide guidance
- Traditional healing may supplement Western services
- Language needs (Navajo/English) must be addressed


PART 9: HOW TO SUPPORT NAVAJO EDUCATION
================================================================================

WAYS TO HELP:

Support Navajo schools:
- Donate books and supplies (contact schools directly)
- Sponsor a student's scholarship
- Volunteer (if you live nearby)
- Advocate for full BIA funding

Support language preservation:
- Learn about Diné language
- Support Diné language programs
- Donate to language immersion schools
- Use language learning resources

Support tribal colleges:
- Donate to Diné College or NTU
- Sponsor scholarships
- Volunteer as a tutor (online options)
- Partner for research or exchange

WHAT TO AVOID:

DO NOT:
- Show up uninvited to schools (privacy concerns)
- Assume you know what's needed (ask first)
- Send used or inappropriate materials
- Impose outside solutions without community input
- Forget to respect Navajo Nation sovereignty
